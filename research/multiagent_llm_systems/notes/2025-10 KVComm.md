# KVComm: Enabling Efficient LLM Communication through Selective KV Sharing

## One line
A sender LLM passes a receiver LLM the attention key/value pairs of a chosen subset of layers, instead of text or hidden states, so the receiver answers as if it had read the sender's context at a fraction of the cost.

## Method
Problem: two LLMs, a sender that holds a context and a receiver that holds the question, must communicate. Natural-language messages need many decoding steps and lose information in token sampling. Hidden-state methods (CIPHER passes averaged embeddings, AC swaps the receiver's last-token hidden state for the sender's) have their own flaws. The authors show two: the last token's hidden state carries most of the information the output needs (information concentration bias), so overwriting it damages the receiver. Prepending all tokens' hidden states works only if taken from early sender layers and fed to early receiver layers, which costs about as much as concatenating the two inputs; fed to later receiver layers, performance drops sharply.

Idea: share KV pairs. The sender runs one prefill pass, no decoding. For each selected layer, its keys and values are concatenated in front of the receiver's own at the same layer index. The receiver's hidden states are untouched, and it decides through attention how much to use.

Which layers to send is the new part. Two hypotheses drive the score. H1: intermediate layers hold the most transferable semantic knowledge. H2: layers whose heads put more attention mass on the context tokens are more useful. Each layer gets an attention importance score (mean attention weight on context tokens, normalised to 0-1) blended with a Gaussian prior centred on a middle layer, weighted by a hyperparameter alpha. The top M layers are sent; layers need not be contiguous. Selection is done once per model pair and dataset on a calibration set and then fixed. The paper reports a single calibration sample matches up to 128 samples (Llama-3.2-3B on three datasets).

Assumption: sender and receiver are the same model or fine-tunes of one base, so layer indices and KV shapes line up.

## Evaluated on
- Matches the no-communication upper bound (receiver reads context plus question directly) at 70% of layers on most datasets, and beats the hidden-state and text baselines (NLD, CIPHER, AC) on long-context QA. Example, Llama-3B pair on HotpotQA: 0.65 at 70% of layers vs 0.73 upper bound, 0.50 CIPHER, 0.43 NLD, 0.05 AC (replace).
- Text and embedding baselines only come close on the two synthetic sets where little, salient information must move; elsewhere the sender sees the whole context but not the question, so text cannot pick out what matters.
- Cost: 2.5x to 6x fewer FLOPs than the upper bound, 23% to 73% less memory on Tipsheets, and up to 3x less transmitted than sending all layers.
- Selection beats random: at 30% of layers on the Llama-3B pair, Countries 0.46 vs 0.05 and MuSiQue 0.28 vs 0.01; the gap shrinks at 70%.
- Beats a single contiguous chunk of layers (DroidSpeak-style), which works only in a small region of the (start, end) hyperparameter space; contiguous chunks do best in intermediate layers, supporting H1. Higher-attention layers do better than low ones, supporting H2.
- It sometimes exceeds the upper bound, which the authors attribute to selective sharing acting as regularisation and to the sender complementing the receiver. These are explanations only; no test of them is reported.

## Limitations admitted by the authors
- Requires identical or same-base models, because KV structure differs across families; heterogeneous pairs are left to future projections or adapters.
- At 30% of layers the gain is unreliable: the Qwen2.5-7B pair scores 0.02 on HotpotQA vs 0.53 for the upper bound, and only 0.28 at 50%.
- Little gain on TMATH (math with hints, 0.35 vs 0.34-0.36 for everything), attributed to pretraining already covering the maths.
- Layers are fixed after calibration, not chosen per input; online adaptive selection is only demonstrated in an appendix.
- Gaussian prior chosen for simplicity; entropy-weighted or learned priors are untried.
- Only one sender and one receiver; a two-sender experiment is preliminary and scaling to larger agent networks is future work.

## What it leaves untried
- Cross-architecture exchange (different families or sizes); real agent teams mix models, so same-base is a narrow case.
- Multi-turn or multi-agent dialogue: only one-shot context-to-question transfer is tested, so the effect on iterative agent loops is unknown.
- Comparison with agentic text summaries tuned to the question; the text baselines get no question, so part of the gap may come from that.
