# Cooperation, Competition, and Maliciousness: LLM-Stakeholders Interactive Negotiation

## One line
A scorable six-party, five-issue negotiation game that measures how well LLM agents cooperate, compete and resist a greedy or sabotaging teammate.

## Method
The problem: LLMs are being put into multi-agent setups, but there is little way to measure their communication and decision-making there. Two-player negotiation tests are too easy, for example the malicious player is trivially identifiable.

The idea is a negotiation as a measurable game. Six parties (a proposer p1, a budget holder p2 with veto, beneficiaries, constraint-imposers, opponents) negotiate five issues with 3 to 5 sub-options each, 720 possible deals. Each party holds a secret score per sub-option and a minimum acceptance threshold. A deal is feasible only if at least 5 parties, including p1 and p2, clear their thresholds. Because scores are non-zero-sum and partly opposed, the feasible set is small (about 55 deals) and success is computable without a human or LLM judge. Difficulty is tuned by changing thresholds; new games can be generated by an LLM and hand-curated.

Agents see shared project information, their own secret scores, and the last 6 public messages. After 24 rounds p1 proposes a final deal that decides the outcome. A structured chain-of-thought gives each turn a hidden scratchpad (score previous deals, infer others' preferences, generate candidates, select) and a hidden plan fed to the agent's next turn.

Three variants are set only through natural-language incentives, never through which deals to propose: compromising, greedy (own score weighted far above others), and adversarial (rewarded when no deal is reached, told to isolate one party, targeted or untargeted). Beyond success, the metrics are own score versus an oracle collective score (do the proposals fit the assigned incentive?), wrong deals (below own threshold, a check on arithmetic), and leakage of secret scores.

## Evaluated on
- The task is hard for strong models. GPT-4 with the best prompt reaches 81% final success, but only 33% when all six must agree. GPT-3.5 reaches 20%.
- Success falls as the feasible set shrinks. GPT-4 drops from 81% (55 five-way deals) to 65% (30) and 30% (17).
- Prompt structure matters, and more is not better. For GPT-4, dropping the candidate-generation step raised success from 15% (all steps) to 45%, because agents drifted toward their own higher-scoring deals. Dropping "infer others' preferences" cut success from 81% to 60%; dropping the plan cut it from 81% to 28%.
- Open models sit between GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. Llama2-70b 76%, Mixtral 65%, Llama3-70b 60%, Gemini Pro 45%, Llama2-13b 57%. Llama3-70b is closest to GPT-4 on wrong deals (4% vs 1.4%) and leakage (2% vs 0%); GPT-3.5 leaks scores in 25% of answers and proposes sub-threshold deals 19% of the time.
- A weak model drags the group down. Swapping p1 to GPT-3.5 cuts all-GPT-4 success from 81% to 50%.
- One bad actor works. A greedy p1 cuts success to 27%. An untargeted adversary lowers it to 63% and a targeted one to 58%. Compromising agents often echo the majority and counter the intruder, but a targeted attack is more effective when the adversary aligns with p1 and p2.

## Limitations admitted by the authors
- Not solved. GPT-4 still underperforms at higher difficulty and on games with non-sparse payoffs (games 1 and 2 score 65% and 70% against 86% for the sparse game 3), where fine-grained deal proposals are needed.
- Some models cannot play at all. Mistral 7b and Llama2 7b were excluded for failing the basic format, so the benchmark says little about small models.
- Only one adversary and one attack type are studied, so that success conditions stay comparable. Private communication, evasion of a moderator and defenses are named as future work.
- Even GPT-4 explores poorly: it sometimes compensates by over-raising its score on one issue rather than finding a balanced deal.

## What it leaves untried
- Defenses. A moderator agent that detects and penalizes the intruder is proposed, not built. The paper reports that compromising agents sometimes resist the intruder but gives no mechanism for it.
- Private channels and deception between parties. All messages are public, so coalition-building and secret manipulation are untested.
- Better planning or reasoning methods than hand-written chain-of-thought. The prompt ablation is on a single game (and mostly GPT-4), so how far it transfers to the new games is not shown.
