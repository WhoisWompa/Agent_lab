# Multi-Agent Collaboration via Cross-Team Orchestration

## One line
Croto runs several independent ChatDev-style agent teams on the same task, pauses them at key phases, merges their drafts into one better draft with an aggregator agent, and hands it back to every team.

## Method
Problem: a single role-based team (instructor and assistant per phase, phases in a chain) produces one outcome per phase, so it follows one decision path and can repeat the same error with no way to self-correct. Running n teams and ensembling the final results wastes the intermediate phases, and teams that share everything converge on the same features.

Idea: keep the teams independent and make them meet only at key phases (coding and code completion for software, after writing for stories). At a meeting:
- **Greedy pruning.** A quality score drops a set share of the weakest drafts, so one aggregator is not asked to merge noise.
- **Hierarchy partitioning.** The remaining drafts are split into small groups. Each group is merged by an aggregator agent that has an assigned role, then the merged outputs are regrouped and merged again until one remains. The reason is that one aggregator cannot hold many drafts in context.
- **Aggregation.** The aggregator lists each draft's strengths and weaknesses, builds a draft that keeps the strengths and drops the weaknesses, and states what it changed. This draft replaces every team's own draft for the later phases.

Teams are made diverse through different temperatures and chain lengths. The authors argue (an informal Zipf-style argument, not tested) that more teams raise the chance that a rare useful feature appears in at least one draft. No task-specific graph is built, unlike graph-based systems.

Note: pruning uses the same Quality metric that the paper reports as its result (footnote 2), so the pruned result is partly selected by the score it is judged on.

## Evaluated on
- Better software than single-team and graph baselines: Quality 0.840 vs 0.779 for ChatDev, 0.759 AgentVerse, 0.710 GPTSwarm, 0.545 MetaGPT (15 software tasks, GPT-3.5-Turbo). Executability rises from 0.813 to 0.928. Completeness is slightly below GPTSwarm (0.795 vs 0.800).
- More teams is not monotonic without pruning. Quality peaks at 4 teams (0.789) and falls at 8 (0.775), which the authors attribute to the aggregator failing to merge too many drafts. Pruning at 8 teams restores 0.840.
- Both mechanisms matter, but unevenly. Removing partitioning at 4 teams gives 0.789 to 0.756, but at 8 teams only 0.775 to 0.773. Removing the aggregator's role gives 0.735 (4 teams) and 0.744 (8 teams).
- Diversity must be tuned. Equal low or equal high temperatures across 4 teams give Quality 0.762 and 0.716, and a mixed (0.2, 0.2, 0.4, 0.4) setting gives 0.795, against 0.789 for the vanilla 4-team run.
- Gains carry to stories (10 ROCStories tasks, scale 0 to 4): 3.083 with 8 teams vs 2.358 for a single team and 2.193 for a single agent, and 3.642 with pruning. Ablations there are larger than for software (8 teams: 3.083 to 2.456 without partitioning).
- Cost: 8 teams use 2.14 times the tokens and 3.558 times the wall time of one team.

## Limitations admitted by the authors
- Pruning relies on an imperfect quality metric and may discard a creative draft that would have improved with more work; no better automated evaluator is offered.
- Agents default to the simplest logic when the task is underspecified (a rectangle for the bird in Flappy Bird, hard-coded data instead of a database), and overly complex story requirements also hurt. Output suits prototypes, not real systems.
- Coordination and interaction load grow with the number of teams, which may make very large or resource-limited use impractical. The cost table shows tokens and time rising with every added team.

## What it leaves untried
- Checking the result with a score the pruner never saw (execution tests, human raters). The paper prunes and evaluates with the same Quality metric, and does not say how story quality was scored.
- A model other than GPT-3.5-Turbo, or more than 15 software tasks and 10 stories. Whether the gain survives a stronger base model is unsupported here.
- Comparing against a cheaper equal-compute baseline, such as best-of-n or a plain ensemble of the same number of single-team runs. The paper argues against ensembling in prose but reports no such run.
