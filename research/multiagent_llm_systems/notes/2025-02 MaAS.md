# Multi-agent Architecture Search via Agentic Supernet

## One line
MaAS learns a distribution over multi-agent workflows (an "agentic supernet") and samples a different, cheaper-or-richer system for each query, instead of searching for one fixed system.

## Method
Problem: automated workflow search (ADAS, AgentSquare, AFlow, GPTSwarm) returns a single complex system per benchmark. That has two costs. It spends the same heavy budget on easy and hard queries (the paper contrasts elementary arithmetic, fine with one zero-shot call, with PhD-level algebra). And on mixed-domain suites like GAIA no single system fits both file reading and web search.

Idea: borrow supernet training from neural architecture search. The search space is a layered cascade (L = 4 in the experiments). Each layer holds "agentic operators", which are packaged LLM-call procedures such as I/O, CoT, self-consistency, ensemble, ReAct, Refine and Debate, plus an early-exit operator. A controller, an MoE-style scorer over the embedded query and the operators chosen in earlier layers, activates operators in score order until their cumulative score passes a threshold (0.3). So the number of operators per layer depends on the query. If early exit is drawn, the chain stops and deeper layers are never paid for.

Training maximises answer quality minus a token-cost penalty (lambda). Execution is non-differentiable, so the distribution parameters are updated with a Monte Carlo estimate. Each of K sampled systems is weighted by its normalised success minus its normalised cost, which pushes probability toward systems that are right and cheap. The operators themselves (prompts, temperature, node structure such as merging or splitting) are improved by an LLM-written "textual gradient" from the execution feedback. Distribution and operators are therefore evolved together.

It should work because the cost term and early exit give the controller a reason to route easy queries to shallow systems. The textual gradient keeps the operator pool from being frozen at hand-written versions.

## Evaluated on
- Beats the best baseline on average accuracy with gpt-4o-mini: 83.59 vs 82.25 for AFlow across GSM8K, MATH, MultiArith, HumanEval and MBPP. The abstract claims 0.54 to 16.89 points over handcrafted and automated systems.
- Much cheaper to train and run on MATH at equal or better accuracy (51.82 vs 51.28 for AFlow). Training cost is $3.38 vs $22.50 (6.8x less). Inference cost is $0.42 vs $1.66. Abstract: 6 to 45% of baseline inference cost.
- Largest gap on the mixed-domain GAIA: 20.69 average vs 16.61 for TapeAgent and 16.34 for AgentSquare. AFlow scores 8.00, which the authors read as the failure of one fixed system across domains.
- Query-dependent behaviour is visible. For two easy MATH queries the early-exit operator is drawn at layer 2 with probability 0.37 and 0.47; harder queries get more layers.
- Ablations: removing the textual gradient costs the most (MATH 51.82 to 48.23, HumanEval 92.85 to 90.17). Removing early exit or the cost term barely changes accuracy but raises MATH cost per query (0.86 to 1.04 and 1.28, in units of 10^-3 $).
- Transfers to Qwen-2.5-70b with 4.98 to 5.50% gains. A held-out Debate operator is still used at a reasonable rate, which the authors call "inductive".

## Limitations admitted by the authors
- The paper has no limitations section, so these come from its own sensitivity runs.
- Larger lambda gives cheaper systems but lower accuracy, so the cost/quality trade-off is a hand-set knob.
- Going from L = 2 to 4 layers lifts accuracy (89.5 to 92.8) but further depth gives only marginal gains at higher per-query cost. The depth cap is chosen empirically.
- With K = 2 sampled systems per query the gradient estimate is suboptimal and has the highest variance. K = 4 was needed.

## What it leaves untried
- Routing quality is never measured on its own. Early exit is shown on hand-picked examples, but there is no number for how often easy queries are over-routed or hard ones under-routed.
- Transfer was tried across datasets, models and one held-out operator, but not to queries from a new task type. The "inductive" claim rests on a single held-out operator.
- Operators sit in a fixed layered DAG. Richer communication among agents beyond sequential layers is not explored.
