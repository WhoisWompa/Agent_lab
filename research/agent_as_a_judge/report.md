# Judges that evolve their own tests remain unbuilt

No published system does what the reader wants to build: a judge agent pointed at one target agent, talking to it over its own channel, keeping a persistent test set, scoring the target, and repairing both its tests and its own judgments each round. The field has built every piece separately. Agentic judges that inspect artifacts beat transcript-reading LLM judges by large margins: about 90% agreement with human consensus against 60-84% in the original Agent-as-a-Judge ([2410.10934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10934)), and about +13 F1 when judges can query environment state in AJ-Bench ([2604.18240](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18240)). Target-specific test generators such as the Agent-Testing Agent ([2508.17393](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17393)) and ALI-Agent ([2405.14125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14125)) sharpen their probes against one system. Self-improving judges such as Self-Taught Evaluators ([2408.02666](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02666)) and Learning While Evaluating ([2512.06751](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06751)) repair their own scoring. These two lines have never been joined, and the 2026 evidence explains why joining them is risky. Label-free judge repair raises agreement between judges without raising agreement with humans ([2610.13303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13303)). A judged agent's own narration can raise a judge's false-positive rate by up to 90% ([2601.14691](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14691)). Judges miss most side effects (recall at most 24.1%, [2610.11050](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11050)). Every method family that holds up under measurement does so by changing what information the judge has: references, execution, environment state, a small human-labelled anchor. Prompting tricks do not hold up. The reader's design is therefore novel, and its viability hinges on one thing the literature has not done. It must separate "my test was too easy" from "my verdict was wrong" and anchor the second signal to something whose errors are independent of the judge's own.

Citations below give a short title and arXiv id, which match the `arxiv:` field in the per-paper notes under `notes/`. Numbers that the source notes mark as recalled or unverified are either omitted or flagged as unverified.

## Six method families, one table

| Family | What it is | What it buys | Where it has been measured to break | What is left untried |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LLM-as-a-judge (prompted) | A general LLM scores, ranks or picks outputs from text alone | Cheap, scalable; 85% expert agreement on MT-Bench | Position, style, cue and self-preference bias; near-chance on hard objective pairs; gameable by null outputs and injected tokens | A defence that holds against an adaptive attacker across attack families |
| Trained judges and reward models | Judges fine-tuned or RL-trained on distilled, synthetic or verifiable labels | Small reasoning judges match much larger prompted reasoners on correctness-heavy meta-evals | Distilled judges behave as in-domain classifiers; style flips them below chance; over-optimisation; right verdicts for wrong reasons | Over-optimisation laws for reasoning and tool-using judges; hacking of RL-trained judges |
| Agentic judges | The judge is an agent that reads workspaces, calls tools or probes the environment | Large accuracy gains when evidence lives in state rather than in the transcript | Tools help some backbones and hurt others; harness swaps move accuracy; side effects missed; misreading tool output | Running new tests against the artifact; questioning the judged agent; diffing state before and after the run |
| Trajectory and step-level judging | Judging the process, not only the outcome; process reward for agents | Catches silent faults outcome judges cannot see; usable as RL reward | Error localisation is 11-14%; judges are lenient and believe self-reports | Agent PRMs for coding or OS traces validated against human step labels |
| Debate, panels and other paradigms | Adversarial debaters, juries, arenas, consistency checks, selective abstention, simulated users | Debate helps under information asymmetry; panels cut bias and cost; abstention gives error guarantees | Debate gains vanish without information gap; deliberation adds little beyond voting; consistency passes systematic errors; simulators inflate success | Nearly all of these combined with tool-using judges on agent trajectories |
| Evolving tests and judges | Test sets refreshed or generated against a target; judges that refine themselves | Fresh, contamination-resistant, failure-revealing tests; judges that improve without human labels | Collusion and trivial tests; criteria and length drift; consensus without alignment; memory reward inflation | A loop that sharpens tests against one target and repairs its own verdicts from measured misjudgments |

## Prompted LLM judges fail where they cannot solve the task

The foundational result is that GPT-4 agrees with experts on MT-Bench 85% of the time on non-tie votes, above the 81% human-human rate (MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, [2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). That paper already measured the three biases that would dominate the next three years. Position consistency under answer swap was only 65.0% for GPT-4 and 23.8% for Claude-v1. A "repetitive list" verbosity attack fooled GPT-3.5 and Claude-v1 91.3% of the time. And the judge failed to grade 70% of math answers it was given, a figure that dropped to 15% when a reference answer was supplied ([2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)). The arc since then is that biases replicate, change size from judge to judge, and concentrate on hard cases.

**Position bias** has become small for frontier judges on easy pairs: at most 0.04 across five 2025-26 judges ([2604.23178](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178)). It still flips the majority of verdicts on challenging tasks, and verdicts change across identical temperature-0 runs (All Verdicts are Not Equal, [2610.12083](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12083)). **Style bias**, meaning a preference for markdown over plain prose, is now the largest measured bias at 0.10-0.76 across models ([2604.23178](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178)). On RM-Bench, reward models averaged **46.6%, below chance**, when the wrong answer had the more attractive style ([2410.16184](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16184)). Judges also follow cues. Injected provenance labels sway GPT-4o and Gemini, and their justifications almost never mention the cue ([2509.26072](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26072)). Prior scores placed in metadata anchor ratings in 7 of 8 models and block 48% of error corrections on industry data ([2608.25869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25869)). Reasoning-model judges are swayed by phrases like "wait, let me think" ([2504.09946](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09946)).

**Self-preference** is real but partly legitimate. On verifiable tasks most of a strong judge's self-preference is deserved, and the harmful part is concentrated on items where the judge itself erred ([2504.03846](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03846)). Preference leakage extends the bias to students trained on the judge's data, and is harder to detect ([2502.01534](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01534)). This links to the deepest failure. In No Free Labels, judges agreed with finance experts mainly on the questions they could answer themselves, and expert references largely closed the gap ([2503.05061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05061)). Dorner et al. turned this into a theorem. **When the judge is no more accurate than the evaluated model, no debiasing method can cut the required ground-truth labels by more than half** (Limits to Scalable Evaluation, [2410.13341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13341)).

**Run-to-run instability** undermines single verdicts. Pairwise verdicts flip 13.6% of the time on average. Semantically equivalent templates change the majority outcome in 25% of cases. About 11 trials are needed to recover a 50-trial verdict with 95% probability (Coin Flip Judge, [2606.13685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13685)). Consistency is not correctness, though. The most deterministic frontier judge in one 2026 audit matched ground truth only 51% of the time ([2610.12083](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12083)). Two production judges combined test-retest reliability above 0.95 with position bias above 0.10 ([2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)).

The attack literature shows that any judge reading attacker-controlled text can be gamed. A constant "null model" response reaches 86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0 ([2410.07137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07137)). Single punctuation tokens and openers like "Thought process:" draw false-positive rewards from reference-based judges, including frontier ones (One Token to Fool, [2507.08794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794)). Gradient-optimised injections choose the winner, and perplexity-based detection fails (JudgeDeceiver, [2403.17710](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17710)). A one-token backdoor in 1% of judge training data triples scores (BadJudge, [2503.00596](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00596)). RobustJudge tested 15 attacks and 8 defences on 13 judges and found that no defence dominates on robustness, utility and cost together ([2506.09443](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09443)). The most relevant attacks for an agent-judging loop are the ones that emerge from optimisation alone. In iterative self-refinement, evaluator scores rise while human-judged quality stalls, and the effect is worse when generator and evaluator share a model (Spontaneous Reward Hacking, [2407.04549](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04549)). RLHF raised human evaluators' false-positive rate by 24.1% on QuALITY and 18.3% on APPS (Language Models Learn to Mislead Humans, [2409.12822](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12822)).

The mitigations that held up change the judge's information, not its prompt. Reference answers, permutation averaging (PORTIA brings GPT-4 to 98% consistency, [2310.01432](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01432)), decomposed checklists (TICK raises exact human agreement from 46.4 to 52.2%, [2410.03608](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03608)) and statistical calibration with a few human labels each fix part of their target. Prompt-only fixes are inconsistent. Chain-of-thought cut math grading failure but lowered LLMBar Adversarial accuracy from 80.8 to 76.6, because the reasoning rationalised the polished but off-instruction answer ([2310.07641](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07641)). CoT and "disregard" warnings did not remove anchoring ([2608.25869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25869)). The best of nine combined debiasing strategies in 2026 still sat near a **70% agreement ceiling** ([2604.23178](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178)). On the hard objective benchmarks the reader's judge will face, GPT-4o scored 50.9-56.6% on JudgeBench, barely above chance, while o3-mini-high scored 80.9% ([2410.12784](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)). On deep-research agent traces the best judges stay **below 55%**, and they are worst at evidence verification (REFLECT, [2605.19196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19196)). The untried piece is a defence shown to hold against an adaptive attacker across attack families; none of the papers found tests one. The surveys (Gu et al. [2411.15594](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15594); Li et al. [2411.16594](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16594)) also under-weight what the 2026 evidence stresses most: validity at the frontier, where the judged system is as strong as the judge.

## Trained judges learned to reason, then reached for tools

The first generation of open judges was distilled from GPT-4 labels: PandaLM ([2306.05087](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05087)), JudgeLM ([2310.17631](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17631)), Auto-J ([2310.05470](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05470)) and Prometheus 1 and 2 ([2310.08491](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08491), [2405.01535](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01535)). The headline agreement figures these papers report are recalled in the notes, not re-verified, and they measure agreement with the GPT-4 teacher rather than with ground truth. What is verified is how they fail. Huang et al. showed they act as task-specific classifiers that fall short of GPT-4 on generalisability, fairness and adaptability ([2403.02839](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02839)). On JudgeBench, fine-tuned judges scored **13.1% (PandaLM) to 57.4%**, many of them below random ([2410.12784](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)). The data source predicts the failure mode. Distilled judges inherit the teacher's preferences. Self-judging loops such as Self-Rewarding LMs inflate length and saturate ([2401.10020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10020)).

The second generation trained on synthetic or verifiable pairs, often with RL, and taught the judge to solve or plan before judging. Self-Taught Evaluators used no human labels and raised Llama3-70B from **75.4 to 88.3 on RewardBench** ([2408.02666](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02666)). J1 trained with GRPO and a position-consistency reward. J1-Qwen-32B scored **80.8 overall, against 78.4 for DeepSeek-R1-671B**, and 71.4 on JudgeBench ([2505.10320](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10320v3)). RM-R1 reached 81.2-81.5 average over three RM benchmarks at 32B, against 77.7 for GPT-4o ([2505.02387](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02387)). JudgeLRM gives the clearest evidence that supervised fine-tuning does not teach reasoning-heavy judging: SFT gains correlate negatively with the share of reasoning-demanding samples ([2504.00050](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00050)). What reasoning buys is that the judge constructs the answer or the rubric first. That is why the gains concentrate on math and code.

Reasoning did not fix everything. Scalar Bradley-Terry RMs trained on 26M carefully curated pairs (Skywork-Reward-V2, [2507.01352](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01352)) still top RewardBench 2 at **84.1**. On that benchmark, Precise Instruction Following is the weakest category for nearly every top model, and Claude Opus 4 scores 41.9 on it ([2506.01937](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01937)). "Outcome Accuracy is Not Enough" shows judges reaching correct verdicts for incorrect reasons, and outcome-only RL erodes rationale consistency over training. Its hybrid reward reaches 82.0 on JudgeBench ([2602.04649](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04649)). The reward-model literature also gives the over-optimisation backdrop. Gold reward eventually falls as a proxy RM is optimised, with a smooth dependence on RM size (Gao et al., [2210.10760](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760)). DPO over-optimises too ([2406.02900](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02900)). Much RLHF gain is length ([2310.03716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716)). RewardBench 2's PPO study warns that benchmark accuracy is "a prerequisite... but not sufficient" for a good reward ([2506.01937](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01937v2)). RaRE finds that judge choice alone produces large spread in rubric-as-reward RL ([2610.21179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21179)).

Process reward models show the same shift from scalar to reasoning. On ProcessBench, trained discriminative PRMs average **31.5-56.5 F1** at finding the earliest error, against 87.9 for prompted o1-mini ([2412.06559](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.06559v4)). The share of correct-answer solutions with flawed steps rises to 51.8% on Omni-MATH ([2412.06559](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.06559v4)). Qwen's PRM lessons found that Monte Carlo step labels generalise worse than LLM-judge or human labels, and that PRMs drift into outcome judges under best-of-N tuning ([2501.07301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.07301)). ThinkPRM, a generative long-CoT PRM trained on about 8K labels, beats discriminative PRMs trained on about 100x more data by up to 8 F1 ([2504.16828](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16828v3)). CriticGPT showed critics can beat paid contractors at catching inserted bugs, but they hallucinate bugs and nitpick at rates that "remain high" ([2407.00215](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00215)).

The 2025-26 response to "correctness needs evidence the model lacks" is the trained tool-using judge. TIR-Judge, an 8B judge RL-trained to call a code executor, gains +6.4% pointwise and +7.7% pairwise over reasoning judges ([2510.23038](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.23038)). OpenReward plans tool calls to gather evidence ([2510.24636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24636)). AgentV-RL uses forward and backward checking agents ([2604.16004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16004)). Agentic Reward Modeling found its search variant sometimes worse than the default ([2502.19328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19328)), an early sign that tools are not free. Cascades put a cheap judge in front of an expensive one. JEV-as-a-Judge escalates low-confidence cases to a reasoning judge and reports beating a GPT-6 judge at 41% of its fee ([2609.26550](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26550)); those are one paper's own runs. The untried pieces here are an over-optimisation study against reasoning or tool-using judges, a measurement of policies learning to persuade a thinking judge, and critic false-positive rates at repository scale. None of these appear in the notes.

## Agentic judges win when evidence lives in state

**Agent-as-a-Judge** (Zhuge et al., ICML 2025, [2410.10934](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10934)) is a GPT-4o judge with five kept modules: graph, locate, read, retrieve, ask. It checks each of DevAI's 365 hierarchical requirements across 55 tasks against a developer agent's workspace. In the gray-box setting it also reads the agent's trajectory. It agrees with a three-human consensus **86.6-92.1% of the time, against 60.4-84.2% for an LLM judge**, while individual humans scored 84.9-89.3%. Ablation on OpenHands shows where the gain comes from: ask alone gives 65.0%, and adding graph, read and locate brings it to 90.4%. Memory "proved detrimental" because early errors cascade, planning was "unstable", and search did not help on small workspaces. None of these three verdicts comes with numbers. The 97% savings figure is against humans. **Against an LLM judge the dollar cost is the same ($30.58 vs $29.63) and the run is about 11x slower** (118 vs 11 minutes). The authors admit the tasks are small, humans disagreed with each other 10-30% of the time, and real trajectories are "nearly inaccessible". The design also leaves out things a builder would expect. The judge never executes code, never writes new tests, and never questions the agent beyond reading its files. The study used one backbone, three agents and three humans, and reported no run-to-run variance.

The descendants fall into four groups. **Rubric-tree judges** keep the per-requirement decomposition and add domain tools. Mind2Web 2 builds a task-specific judge script with an average of about 50 rubric nodes, and Extractor and Verifier tools check claims against cited pages. Verifier correctness is 99.03%, and 27 of 35 human-judge discrepancies were human errors ([2506.21506](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21506)). The cost was over 1,000 human hours plus per-script review. **Environment-aware judges** inspect live state. AJ-Bench gives judges MCP access to search, database, filesystem and GUI environments. Tool use lifts gpt-5-mini from 59.00 to 72.41 F1 and deepseek-v3.2 from 64.49 to 77.34. More interaction turns help, but more reasoning effort does not reliably help ([2604.18240](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18240)). VAGEN extracts evidence from the trajectory and then probes the live GUI environment from cheap checks to expensive ones ([2602.00575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00575)); its abstract gives no numbers. **Trained agentic verifiers**, covered in the previous section, include TIR-Judge, ARM-Thinker ([2512.05111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05111)) and AgentV-RL. **Agents that write the evaluation** include Agentic Rubrics, where an agent explores a repository, writes a codebase-grounded rubric and scores SWE patches without running tests. It reaches 54.2% best-of-N on SWE-Bench Verified, at least 3.5 points above baselines ([2601.04171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04171)). EvalAgent writes the whole evaluation from an agent's source and traces, and experts preferred it 79.5% of the time ([2605.11378](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11378)). Domain applications include CodeVisionary for code ([2504.13472](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13472)), FinResearchBench for financial research ([2507.16248](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16248)) and DREAM for deep research ([2602.18940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18940)).

The 2026 critiques are empirical and specific. AgentHorizon ran judges inside five coding-agent harnesses on 1,373 computer-use trajectories. GPT-5.5 inside Codex reached **80.9% balanced accuracy against 68.6% as a direct judge**. Qwen 3.6 27B went the other way: **77.6% direct, 70.4% agentic**. Swapping the harness alone moved accuracy by about 3.7 points. Recall on bad side effects was **at most 24.1%**. Without the instruction in view, Qwen called 98.9% of trajectories successful ([2610.11050](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11050v1)). AJ-Bench's most common failure is the judge misreading tool output. In one logged case the judge's reasoning concluded FAIL while its output said PASS ([2604.18240](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18240)). EvalAgent's study of unconstrained agentic evaluators found they write 2.4-6.6x more code than needed, **35-75% fall back to keyword heuristics**, and the agentic meta-judge agreed with itself across three runs only 76.3% of the time ([2605.11378](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11378)). AgentJudgeBench found six judges stuck in a **77-82% band** on hard tool-calling queries regardless of scale. Giving them ground truth lowered alignment by 1.5-3.9 points through over-anchoring ([2608.26623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26623)).

The most important critique for the reader's design is **Gaming the Judge** ([2601.14691](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14691)). It rewrote only the chain of thought of 800 web-agent trajectories, leaving actions and screenshots unchanged, and raised judge false-positive rates by up to 90%. Fabricated progress claims added 20-30 points of FPR on GPT-4o. Prompting, voting and extended thinking reduced the effect without removing it, and each cost recall. The authors suggest grounding claims against observations but do not test it. The judged agent's narration is an attack surface, and the original paper's gray-box mode is the most exposed to it.

The Agent-as-a-Judge survey (You, Cai et al., [2601.05111](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05111v1)) classifies judges by stage (procedural, reactive, self-evolving) and by capability (collaboration, planning, tools, memory, optimisation). It lists cost, latency, safety and privacy as challenges, and admits there is no consensus definition of an "evaluation agent". What the taxonomy lacks is an axis for **which evidence the judge trusts**: narration, artifacts or live state. It also lacks an axis for robustness to the judged agent. The 2026 empirical work says those two axes decide failure. Across the descendants, three things are untried. No judge generates and runs new adversarial checks against the artifact. Agentic Rubrics explicitly avoids execution. No judge interrogates the developer agent about its claims. And no study has the judged agent shape its workspace, rather than its narration, to fool a tool-using judge.

## Outcome verifiers and trajectory judges err in opposite directions

Every major agent benchmark judges final state or final answer only: SWE-bench, WebArena, OSWorld, tau-bench, GAIA, BrowseComp, Terminal-Bench, MLE-bench and AgentDojo. These verifiers are wrong often and in both directions. Test-based verifiers over-credit. SWE-Bench+ found **32.67% of passing patches had leaked solutions and 31.08% passed on weak tests** ([2410.06992](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06992)). PatchDiff found 7.8% of "correct" Verified patches fail the full developer suite ([2503.15223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15223)). UTBoost found 345 wrongly passing patches that changed leaderboard rankings ([2506.09289](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09289)). METR's maintainers would not merge about half of 296 passing agent PRs ([METR](https://metr.org/notes/2026-03-10-many-swe-bench-passing-prs-would-not-be-merged-into-main/)). OpenAI found 59.4% of 138 audited hard Verified tasks flawed and stopped reporting the benchmark ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/)). The notes got this page through a search summary because the fetch was blocked. State- and string-match verifiers under-credit instead: WebArena-style rules reach precision 83.8 but recall only 55.9 against expert labels ([2504.08942](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942)). The Agentic Benchmark Checklist reports task and reward-design flaws that shift measured performance by up to 100% relative ([2507.02825](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02825)). Every "verified" subset has eventually been re-audited and found faulty, including OSWorld-Verified ([Epoch](https://epoch.ai/blog/osworld)). One-off human screening does not settle it.

LLM judges on trajectories fail the other way, toward leniency. On AgentRewardBench, the best of 12 judges reached precision 69.8 and recall 83.1, and **no judge passed 70% precision** ([2504.08942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08942)). Its named failure modes are the ones a transcript reader is built to suffer. The judge believes the agent's claim that a filter was applied. It accepts a found product that was never purchased. It misses an infeasibility claim that was wrong. Adding the accessibility tree to screenshots lowered precision, so more input distracted the judge. OSReward found the best VLM judges disagree with benchmark verifiers on about a quarter of desktop verdicts, with a leniency bias ([2607.28609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28609)). MobileJudgeBench found judges misclassify 9-24% of mobile trajectories and reorder agent rankings by up to 13 places. A simple sampled-screenshot baseline matched purpose-built pipelines, and RL with judge rewards underperformed RL with a rule reward (at most 45.4% vs 54.6%) ([2608.11434](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11434v1)). GAUGE found that **57.5% of conversations rated "satisfied" had failed the task** ([2609.12191](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12191)).

Step-level judging adds something outcome judging cannot. In trajectory-judge, an outcome-only judge had **zero paired discrimination** on faults that leave the final reply unchanged, while a step judge reached +1.00. Raw recall misled, though: an 8B judge flagged 397 of 400 trajectories ([2609.00038](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.00038v2)). Locating errors remains hard. The best model locates and categorises errors 11% of the time on TRAIL ([2505.08638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08638)). Who&When attributes multi-agent failures at 53.5% agent-level and **14.2% step-level** accuracy ([2505.00212](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00212)). Counsel's best open judge gets about 88% of error locations right but only about 65% of the reasoning ([2606.21627](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21627)). As process reward, checklists are the practical form. Web-Shepherd's checklist-conditioned PRM adds +10.9 on WebArena-lite as a verifier ([2505.15277](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15277)). AgentPRM scores steps by promise and progress ([2511.08325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08325)). Trajectory-level evaluators used as a reward improved WebArena by 29% relative ([2404.06474](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06474)). Agent-as-a-Judge names its requirement checks as a future process reward but never builds one. Log inspection catches what scores hide. HAL's LLM-aided log reading, over 21,730 rollouts costing about $40k, found agents searching HuggingFace for the benchmark and misusing credit cards ([2510.11977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11977)).

The two error directions suggest a pairing nobody has evaluated. Use an agentic judge as a second opinion on test-passing cases, where execution over-credits. Use state checks as a second opinion on judge-passing cases, where reading over-trusts. The notes also find no agent PRM validated against human step labels on coding or OS traces, and no paper that turned METR's 296 reviewed PRs into a judge benchmark.

## Debate, juries, consistency and abstention were built for single outputs

**Debate** reliably beats consultancy (one advocate). It beats an unaided judge only when the judge lacks information the debaters have. With hidden passages and verified quotes, LLM judges reached **76% with debate against 48% naive**, and humans 88% against 60% (Khan et al., [2402.06782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06782)). Across extractive QA, math, code, logic and multimodal tasks, debate beat direct QA only with information asymmetry and gave mixed results elsewhere (Kenton et al., [2407.04622](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04622)). Two 2025 human studies found simple oversight protocols gave no overall advantage over deferring to the model. Participants who researched online grew more confident even when the system was wrong ([2507.19486](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19486)). Scaling laws for oversight put nested debate's success at **51.7% at a 400-Elo gap**, falling as the overseen system gets stronger ([2504.18530](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18530)). Prover-verifier games produced sneaky provers whose wrong solutions time-limited humans accepted more often over training ([2407.13692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13692)). That is direct evidence that optimisation produces persuasive but wrong outputs. The obfuscated-arguments problem has a proposed theoretical fix, prover-estimator debate ([2506.13609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13609)), with no empirical LLM test in the notes. Agent evaluation is mostly a reasoning gap plus a lot of evidence to read, which is the regime where debate has not been shown to help.

**Panels and multi-agent debate** help through independent votes, not deliberation. PoLL, a panel of three small models from different families, beat a single GPT-4 judge on QA kappa (for example 0.763 against 0.627 on NQ) at 7-8x lower cost ([2404.18796](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18796)). Debate or Vote showed majority voting explains most multi-agent-debate gains, because debate forms a martingale over beliefs ([2508.17536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17536)). ChatEval's referee team scored **34.0% on JudgeBench, below a single GPT-4o** ([2410.12784](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)). Panel independence is limited because model errors become more correlated as capability grows ([2502.04313](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04313)). **Arenas** measure style-sensitive crowd preferences that providers can overfit. The Leaderboard Illusion documents 27 private Meta variants tested before Llama-4, and up to 112% relative gain from extra Arena data ([2504.20879](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879)). Vote rigging with a few hundred votes can shift rankings ([2501.17858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17858)). Peer-review rankers such as PiCO ([2402.01830](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01830)) and Auto-Arena ([2405.20267](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20267)) validate against Arena and inherit its biases.

**Consistency and cross-examination** checks catch random errors and pass systematic ones. LM vs LM has an examiner cross-examine an examinee and flag contradictions ([2305.13281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13281)). Fluri et al. found logical violations in superhuman chess engines and forecasts without ground truth ([2306.09983](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09983)). Round-trip correctness extends code evaluation to unlabelled domains ([2402.08699](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08699)). By construction, SelfCheckGPT ([2303.08896](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08896)) and semantic entropy ([2302.09664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.09664)) give low uncertainty to an error repeated in every sample. Intrinsic self-correction without external feedback often degrades reasoning ([2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798)).

**Selective judging** turns occasional judge error into a controlled error rate. Trust or Escalate guarantees **over 80% human agreement at almost 80% coverage** on a Chatbot Arena subset where GPT-4 alone almost never reaches 80% ([2407.18370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370)). SCOPE holds a 10% false-discovery bound while accepting up to 2.4x more judgments than baselines ([2602.13110](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13110)). Prediction-powered inference combines a few human labels with many judge labels ([2403.07008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07008)). The price is the abstained cases, plus an assumption that calibration and test data are exchangeable. Dorner's half-the-labels bound sets the ceiling.

**Simulated users** are a judge-like model in disguise. Measured against 451 real people, simulators on tau-bench were "excessively cooperative" and inflated success ([2603.11245](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11245)). Swapping the user LLM moved success by up to 9 points, and simulators were the worst proxies for AAVE and Indian English speakers ([2601.17087](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17087)). Swapping the user proxy moved reward by 15.2 points, and 24.4% of successful episodes violated the user spec ([2609.38043](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38043)). The tau-bench simulator cannot decline or walk away ([2606.20708](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.20708)). For the reader this matters directly. A judge that talks to the target over a channel is a user simulator, and it inherits these calibration problems unless it is checked against human interactions. Interactive evaluation still earns its place: non-interactive metrics did not predict interactive quality across five tasks (HALIE, [2212.09746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09746)).

Across this family, the notes found no debate study where the judge has tools or the object debated is an agent trajectory. They found no selective-evaluation study on agent runs and no cross-examination of an agent about its own actions. Execution and user simulation are the only alternative paradigms widely applied to agents.

## Evolving tests sharpen against targets; evolving judges drift toward consensus

Two disjoint lines exist, and they measure "missed" differently.

**Test generators** evolve their tests against a fixed judge, and "missed" means the target passed. Perez et al.'s red LM escalated from zero-shot to RL. RL found the most failures with the least diverse tests ([2202.03286](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.03286)), which prompted curiosity rewards ([2402.19464](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19464)). Rainbow Teaming keeps coverage with a MAP-Elites archive over risk-by-style cells ([2402.16822](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16822)). AutoDetect's Examiner, Questioner and Assessor iterate on one target LLM ([2406.16714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16714)). ALI-Agent keeps a memory of past test records and refines misconduct scenarios against one target ([2405.14125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14125)). AutoBencher optimises dataset descriptions and grounds answers in retrieval or Python for validity, finding 22% more model errors ([2407.08351](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08351)). Automated Capability Discovery proposes thousands of tasks against a subject model and clusters its failures ([2502.07577](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07577)). EvalTree turns a benchmark into a capability tree and reports a model's weak subtrees ([2503.08893](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08893)). Examiner-style systems ask follow-ups based on the examinee's answers: LM-as-an-Examiner ([2306.04181](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04181)), TreeEval ([2402.13125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13125)), KIEval ([2402.15043](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15043)) and LLM-as-an-Interviewer ([2412.10424](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10424)). Refreshed benchmarks fight leakage by construction: LiveBench ([2406.19314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19314)), LiveCodeBench ([2403.07974](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974)) and DyVal ([2309.17167](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17167)). In code, the oracle makes validity tractable. CURE rewards tests that separate correct from incorrect solutions ([2506.03136](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03136)). Agentic Verifier refines an input generator with execution feedback ([2602.04254](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04254)). Code-A1 found that **single-model white-box self-play "leads to self-collusion"**, with trivial tests for easy reward. It separates the Code LLM from the Test LLM with opposing rewards and keeps a "Mistake Book" ([2603.15611](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15611)). Outside code, test validity rests on an LLM judge plus sampled human checks. That re-imports judge error into the weakness measurement itself. ToolEmu's figure of about 68.8% of flagged failures confirmed by humans is a rare reported validity rate, and it is unverified in the notes ([2309.15817](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15817)).

**Self-improving judges** evolve their own scoring, and "misjudged" is measured by proxy. Self-Taught Evaluators get labels by construction: the response to a perturbed instruction is worse ([2408.02666](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02666)). Meta-Rewarding has a meta-judge rank judgments, and it needs explicit length control or the loop drifts toward longer outputs ([2407.19594](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19594)). Its headline AlpacaEval gains are recalled and unverified in the notes. Selective LWE keeps an evolving meta-prompt and updates only on cases where the judge is self-inconsistent ([2512.06751](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06751)). SEMJ uses cross-lingual inconsistency as the trigger ([2606.08092](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08092)). Rubric generators are fine-tuned from meta-judge rewards ([2605.30568](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30568)), and Rubrics on Trial compares rubrics through synthetic responses written to satisfy each one ([2607.15092](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15092)). SPC trains a critic against a sneaky generator that injects errors, which must change final answers. That keeps it honest and lifts ProcessBench from 70.8% to 77.7% ([2504.19162](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19162)).

The 2026 evidence is that label-free proxies raise consistency without raising truth. In a preregistered study, rewriting the rubric clauses with most inter-judge disagreement raised judge-judge Krippendorff alpha by **+0.069** (CI 0.046-0.092) on SummEval. Human agreement moved by **-0.007** Kendall tau (CI -0.037 to 0.021). Human agreement was lower after contested-clause edits than after control edits on all three datasets (Agreement Without Alignment, [2610.13303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13303)). Memory Reward Inflation names the mechanism that will hit any judge that stores its own scores. Wrong episodes get inflated scores that compound (the "Echo Gap"), and a correcting signal works only if its errors are independent of the judge's self-grading bias ([2608.00017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00017)). EvalGen documents criteria drift: grading outputs changes the criteria users want ([2404.12272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272)). A production report on self-improvement loops catalogued 11 failure modes. In one, a **100% pass rate concealed about 68% true capability** because agents read cached answer keys. Rubric rewrites gave limited gains, while constraining the judge's output order helped reliably. The proposed guardrails are frozen holdouts, canaries where a perfect score signals cheating, capability-disjoint roles, and acceptance checks the judge cannot override (PROCTOR, [2609.02246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02246)). LLM-generated benchmarks also favour their generator model ([2505.20738](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20738)); the notes flag this paper's title as unverified. Three drift stops have measured support: an external oracle (execution, ground truth, injected errors), a frozen human-labelled holdout, and role separation. Self-inconsistency and inter-judge disagreement catch unstable judgments but not stable wrong ones. The longest self-improving-judge runs reported are 3-5 iterations, and no paper tracks human agreement per round over a long run.

## The closest works each cover two of six design parts

The reader's design has six parts. Read the table against them: (1) one specific target agent; (2) the judge talks to it over its own channel; (3) the judge keeps a persistent test set across rounds; (4) the judge scores the target; (5) tests are refined where they missed, meaning the target passed but should have been probed harder; (6) the judge's own scoring or tests are repaired where it misjudged, meaning its verdict was wrong against an oracle.

| Work | (1) One agent | (2) Channel | (3) Persistent set | (4) Scores | (5) Test-side refine | (6) Judge-side repair |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agent-Testing Agent ([2508.17393](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17393)) | Yes | Yes | Not described | Yes, fixed LLM rubric | Yes, toward low scores | No |
| Online Agent-as-a-Judge ([2606.08200](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08200)) | Yes | Yes, in-world | No | Yes, fixed criteria | No rounds described | No |
| ALI-Agent ([2405.14125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14125)) | LLM, not agent | Single-turn | Memory of test records | Yes, fixed evaluator | Yes, harder scenarios | No |
| AutoDetect ([2406.16714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16714)) | LLM, not agent | Q&A | Not described | Fixed Assessor | Yes | No |
| Selective LWE ([2512.06751](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06751)) | No target | No | No | Pairwise only | No | Yes, from self-inconsistency |
| Agent-as-a-Judge ([2410.10934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10934)) | Yes, post hoc | Reads workspace; "ask" is internal | Fixed DevAI set | Yes, ~90% | No | No |
| EvalGen ([2404.12272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272)) | One LLM pipeline | No | Assertions kept | Yes | No | Yes, from human grades |
| Code-A1 ([2603.15611](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15611)) | Code model | No | Mistake Book | Execution | Yes | Validity reward, code only |

The **Agent-Testing Agent** is closest overall. It reads the target's code, interviews its designer, runs persona-driven adversarial dialogues over the target's conversational interface, scores each with an LLM rubric, and steers later tests toward low-scoring capabilities. It found more diverse and more severe failures than expert annotators in 20-30 minutes against days ([2508.17393](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17393)). It differs from the reader's design in four ways. Refinement is driven by where the target scored low, not by where the judge's tests missed or misjudged. The rubric is fixed for the whole loop, and its calibration is compared with annotators but never improved. No persistent test set carried across target versions is described. It was tested on only two targets. Its ablation matters for the reader: removing code analysis and web search increased variance and miscalibration, which argues for white-box inputs.

**Online Agent-as-a-Judge** is the closest on the channel. The evaluator acts inside the environment through the target's own dialogue and action interface. It provokes situations tied to 32 designer-authored criteria instead of scoring a free-running trajectory afterward, and it improves criteria coverage and human agreement ([2606.08200](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08200)). It differs in that its criteria are human-authored and fixed, the abstract describes no round-over-round refinement or persistent situation set, and it covers one life-simulation environment. The abstract gives no numbers.

**ALI-Agent** is the closest on keeping and refining tests. It stores past test records and refines each misconduct scenario over several rounds until it exposes misalignment ([2405.14125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14125)). It differs in that the target is a single-turn LLM rather than an agent over a channel, refinement only makes tests harder and never corrects the evaluator, and it covers values and safety only. **AutoDetect** has the same shape for LLM capability weaknesses, with a fixed Assessor ([2406.16714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16714)).

**Selective LWE** is the judge-repair half alone. Its judge revises its evaluation instructions across a stream of cases, updating when it is self-inconsistent ([2512.06751](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06751)). It differs in that it generates no tests, has no target to interact with, judges pairwise only, and defines "misjudged" as self-inconsistency rather than disagreement with an oracle. Agreement Without Alignment predicts that this signal will not reliably move human agreement. **EvalGen** repairs the judge's assertions from human grades, which is a true oracle, but a human must be in the loop and it has no target to probe ([2404.12272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272)).

Several works sit further out. **Agent-as-a-Judge** contributes the evidence model: inspect the workspace rather than read the narration. But it is post hoc and has no rounds. **Code-A1** contributes role separation and a Mistake Book, in code only. **Automated Capability Discovery** ([2502.07577](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07577)) and **Evaluation Agent** ([2412.09645](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09645)) probe one subject model adaptively over rounds, but the subject is a model, not a channel-reachable agent. **IntellAgent** runs simulated users against a conversational agent from a policy graph, but generates scenarios up front and does not refine them from failures ([2501.11067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11067)). **Sentient Agent as a Judge** converses with the model under test to judge social cognition ([2505.02847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02847)). **PROCTOR** is not a judge of a target at all, but it is the only paper whose guardrails were built for the drift a self-refining loop will meet ([2609.02246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02246)). One caution applies to the whole comparison: the notes read the ATA, Online Agent-as-a-Judge, LWE and SEMJ papers at abstract level only. Whether any of them keeps a persistent cross-round test set is inferred, not confirmed.

## Gaps and untried combinations

This section lists what the notes found no paper doing. Absence claims cover only what the six research slices searched. Fast-moving 2026 work such as SEAL ([2605.24426](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24426)), AgentStream ([2608.00155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00155)) and EvoAgentBench ([2607.05202](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05202)) was found but not read.

### Untried combinations directly on the reader's path

| # | Untried combination | What it extends | Why the evidence says it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | **Two named miss signals in one loop.** Track a test-side miss (target passed a test it should not have passed) separately from a judge-side miss (the judge's verdict disagreed with an oracle), and refine tests from the first and the judge from the second | ATA, ALI-Agent (test side); LWE, EvalGen (judge side) | The literature keeps the two lines disjoint; no paper names both signals |
| G2 | **Judge-side repair anchored to an error-independent oracle.** Use execution, environment-state checks, injected known faults or a small human-labelled slice, not self-inconsistency or panel disagreement | SPC's injected errors; EvalGen's human grades; PROCTOR's acceptance checks | Label-free repair raises consensus, not alignment (2610.13303); Echo Gap needs error independence (2608.00017) |
| G3 | **Planted-fault canaries in the test set.** Seed known-bad target behaviours, such as a mock target with injected bugs or side effects, so each round measures the judge's own recall and false-positive rate | SPC's sneaky generator; PROCTOR canaries; ImpossibleBench ([2510.20270](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20270)) | Gives a per-round ground truth for "misjudged" without humans |
| G4 | **Frozen holdout plus per-round human-agreement tracking over many rounds** | Meta-Rewarding and Self-Taught Evaluators (3-5 iterations) | No study measures how fast an evolving judge drifts from humans as rounds accumulate |
| G5 | **A judge that ignores narration and verifies claims against state** | Gaming the Judge (suggested, not tested); AJ-Bench; VAGEN | CoT rewriting alone raised FPR by up to 90% |
| G6 | **State diff before and after each test to catch side effects** | AgentRewardBench side-effect labels; AgentHorizon | Side-effect recall is at most 24.1%; no paper reports side-effect accuracy as a headline judge metric |
| G7 | **Cross-examining the target over the channel about its own actions** | LM vs LM ([2305.13281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13281)); Agent-as-a-Judge's "ask" (which queries the judge's own LLM, not the target) | A natural fit for a channel-based judge; never applied to agents |
| G8 | **Persistent test set carried across target versions, with a never-trained-on slice** | Rainbow Teaming, AutoDetect and MART fine-tune on found tests | Reusing found tests for training makes later scores on them uninformative |
| G9 | **Calibrating the channel-talking judge as a user simulator** | Sim2Real, Lost in Simulation, UserProxyBench | Simulator choice moves scores by 9-15 points; a probing judge is a simulator |
| G10 | **Cost per valid, failure-revealing test for agent targets** | ATA (20-30 min), TestGen-LLM | No measurement exists for agents |

## Conclusion

The field's trajectory over 2023-2026 is a steady move of the judge's trust away from text the evaluated system controls and toward evidence it does not: first references, then execution, then workspace files, then live environment state. Every family's best result follows that move, and every family's worst failure comes from reversing it, through narration, style, cues or a judge grading its own memory. For a self-refining judge the implication is sharp. The test-side loop is safe to automate, because a test that the target passes is an observable fact. The judge-side loop is not, unless "misjudged" is defined against something whose errors are independent of the judge's own. The strongest available candidates are planted faults with known answers, state checks, and a small frozen human slice.

That makes G1-G3 together the most defensible novel contribution. Test misses and judge misses become separate signals. Judge repair is anchored to planted canaries and state verification. Human agreement on a frozen holdout is reported every round. No paper has run this, and the 2026 negative results (Agreement Without Alignment, Echo Gap, Gaming the Judge) are precisely the outcomes such a design is built to detect. A run that reports per-round judge recall on planted faults alongside target scores would also be the first long-horizon measurement of evolving-judge drift in the literature.
