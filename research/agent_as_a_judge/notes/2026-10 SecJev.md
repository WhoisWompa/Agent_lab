# SecJev: Bringing Security Expertise to System One Decision Models

## One line
Fine-tunes Kev's open Jev-style single-pass candidate scorer (0.8B to 9B) on a 14-task security corpus, so that one small model returns typed, probabilistic security decisions (injection present, quorum reached, logon action) and beats a general 9B decision model by 20.51 points.

## Method
The problem: security workflows turn observations (tool responses, traffic, authentication histories, voting messages) plus a stated policy into finite decisions. A general Jev-style model has the right interface (observation, question, candidates in; typed probabilities out) but lacks the domain knowledge to read these observations. General Kev-9B scores only 69.82% task-macro, against 62.89% for always picking the majority class.

The solution keeps Kev's decision path. Each question is one causal row: state, question, delimited candidates, then a decision marker. The hidden state at the marker and the state at each candidate's closing delimiter are projected to 256 dimensions. Their scaled dot product is the candidate's logit, and a softmax over candidates gives the probabilities. Nothing is decoded; candidate descriptions are inputs, so one head serves any answer vocabulary. Security adaptation freezes the backbone and trains only rank-16 LoRA adapters and the pointer head, starting from Kev's general-decision weights. Each question is weighted 1/m_i, where m_i is the number of questions in its scene, so scenes with many questions do not dominate.

The corpus (170,185 questions) mixes two kinds of target. Source-label prediction recovers a dataset's original annotation from the visible telemetry. Explicit-policy evaluation applies a displayed rule to a displayed history. The assumption is that both share one typed interface without interference.

## Evaluated on
- Specialization helps at every size and on all 14 tasks: macro gains of 39.40, 40.83, 26.63 and 24.26 points for 0.8B, 2B, 4B and 9B, all intervals excluding zero. SecJev-0.8B (90.34%) beats Kev-9B (69.82%). The best model is 4B, at 94.51%.
- The gains are uneven by kind of task. At 4B, policy tasks reach 99.87% and tool-text tasks 99.30%, but source-label tasks reach only 85.19%.
- The scorer roughly matches one-token generative SFT on the same data and uses less memory. SecJev scores 90.34/94.51% at 0.8B/4B, against 90.02/94.35% for SecGen. Peak GPU memory drops from 3.52 to 2.56 GiB and from 12.58 to 10.18 GiB, and latency is close.
- Four-shot transfer at 4B, with weights fixed: on BIPIA, 83.60% against 71.30% for Kev and 79.60% for SecGen, though the false-positive rate is still 32.60%. On IoT-23, 90.20% against 73.00% and 84.30%.
- Paraphrasing a question costs at most 0.93 points of aggregate accuracy, but 15 to 38 of 1,400 correct answers flip to wrong.

## Limitations admitted by the authors
- Source-label tasks measure whether the model can predict a dataset's annotation, not whether it can detect attacks. Labels depend on information outside the input: both human reviewers judged the evidence sufficient on only 28 of 100 source-label questions, against 179 of 180 tool and policy questions.
- Task-macro accuracy weights tasks equally, so near-perfect policy scores can hide weak tasks. VeReMi's 6,642 test questions count for only two of the 14 terms, which is why micro accuracy (88.01% at 4B) sits below macro.
- No end-to-end evaluation of attack prevention or response; only finite decisions over supplied observations.
- Confidence degrades even where accuracy does not. Temperature scaling improves NLL but worsens ECE on the security test (9B: 1.53% to 2.48%). On general transfer, calibrated 0.8B ECE is 21.10%, against 4.19% before specialization.
- Leakage control rests on recorded group boundaries. Residual semantic overlap and transfer beyond the tested sources remain open. AgentDojo and InjecAgent have only 2 test groups each, so their intervals are coarse.

## What it leaves untried
- No adversarial test of the security judge itself. Prompt injection against Jev is cited as motivation, yet no injection is aimed at SecJev's own verdicts. That matters most for a model meant to sit on a trust boundary.
- Policy tasks reach about 99.9% using rules generated by the same scripts as the training data. Rules the model has never seen are not tested, so it is unknown whether it applies a policy or has memorized a fixed one.
- Calibration is not fixed for security use: one global temperature worsens ECE. Per-task temperatures or abstention thresholds, which a security deployment acting on probabilities would need, are not tried.
