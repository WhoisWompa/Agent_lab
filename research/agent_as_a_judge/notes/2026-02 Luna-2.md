# Luna-2: Scalable Single-Token Evaluation with Small Language Models

## One line
Turns a small decoder-only LM into many cheap guardrail judges by giving each metric its own LoRA adapter that answers in one token, whose logit gives a confidence score.

## Method
The problem: LLM-as-a-judge is too slow (about 3 s per call with chain-of-thought), too expensive and too non-deterministic to run on all production traffic as a real-time guardrail. The earlier Luna needed a separate DeBERTa encoder and about 100K examples per metric.

The idea: keep the judge generative in form but collapse it to one forward pass. Each metric (toxicity, context adherence, tool selection quality, prompt injection, tone, PII) gets a prompt template that asks a question about the relevant span of a trace (I/O only, LLM span, or LLM span plus tool list) and asks for a one-word answer. Class words are checked to be single vocabulary tokens, or mapped to tokens that are. Training uses cross-entropy only over the class tokens: the full-vocabulary softmax is renormalised over the class set, so the partition function cancels and the score is just a softmax over two (or k) logits. At inference that renormalised probability is the confidence. The base SLM (Llama/Mistral/Qwen, 3B to 8B) is frozen. Each metric is a LoRA adapter at about 3% of base size (r=16, α=16), so hundreds of metrics share one backbone on one GPU.

Why it should work: a narrow expert trained on curated data can match a generalist on a narrow task, and the frozen LM keeps the instruction-following and long-context handling encoders lacked. Labels come partly from a majority vote of closed frontier LLMs, so the judge is partly distilled from frontier judges.

## Evaluated on
- Fine-tuned heads match a GPT-4.1 ChainPoll judge (3 CoT runs averaged): F1 0.95 vs 0.96 on context adherence, 0.94 vs 0.97 on prompt injection, 0.94 vs 0.95 on tool selection quality, 0.92 vs 0.90 on tone, 0.89 vs 0.88 on PII.
- The fine-tuning does the work, not the single-token format: prompting base Llama 3.2 3B for one token gives F1 0.43 on context adherence, 0.57 on tool selection, 0.21 on PII.
- Backbone hardly matters: context-adherence F1 ranges 0.93 to 0.96 across seven Llama, Ministral and Qwen backbones from 3B to 8B.
- Cost and latency: $0.01 per 1K evals and 150 ms vs $3.60 and 3000 ms for GPT-4.1 at about 1250 input tokens. The prose quotes $2.00 for GPT-4.1, so the paper disagrees with its own table on the cost ratio.

## Limitations admitted by the authors
- Latency grows superlinearly at long context: the 3B model on A100 goes from 150 ms at 500 tokens to 1200 ms at 15K and 20000 ms at 100K. That erodes the real-time claim for full agent traces or sessions.
- Each head is a narrow expert; domain shift needs re-tuning on in-house data. The data needed is stated both as 2k to 5k examples and as 30k to 100k per metric, so the real per-domain cost is unclear.
- Misclassification harms and bias inherited from the base model, the data and the rubrics, uneven across groups and languages. No per-group or multilingual numbers are given.
- They recommend calibrated thresholds, abstention and human review for high-stakes use. None of these is tested.

## What it leaves untried
- Measuring calibration. "Calibrated scores" is a stated contribution, but only ROC curves are shown: no ECE or reliability plot. A renormalised two-logit softmax trained with cross-entropy is not calibrated by construction. For guardrail thresholds, calibration is the property that matters.
- Checking against degenerate baselines and under shift. The context-adherence test set is 4.4k adherent vs 0.6k non-adherent. If "adherent" is the positive class, always answering "adherent" gives precision about 0.88, recall 1.00 and F1 about 0.93, and three backbones report exactly P 0.87, R 1.00, F1 0.93. Tone and PII are tested only on proprietary sets, and BFCL was relabelled under the authors' own metric definition. No out-of-distribution or adversarial-robustness test is run.
- Comparing against other cheap trained judges (MiniCheck, Lynx, HHEM, Llama Guard), which the paper cites but never runs, and judging whole multi-step trajectories rather than one span.
