# AnyJev Technical Report: Typed Decisions from One Prefill of Any Open LLM

## One line
Reads a Jev-style typed decision (a probability over a fixed option list) from one prefill of a frozen open instruction-tuned LLM, and corrects the readout's label and position biases without any training.

## Method
- **Problem.** Agents need many small non-text decisions (route, flag injection, score) as thresholdable probabilities, which today means a trained model. The paper's claim: the capability is already in the base model; only the readout is broken.
- **Readout.** Each list position gets a single-token label; one prefill, then the next-token distribution is restricted to those labels and renormalised. The probability mass landing on the labels is a gate: gpt-oss's chat template gave plausible-looking answers at mass 0.000.
- **Label prior.** The model favours some labels regardless of input (Yes, early letters). AnyJev divides out the running mean of its own outputs on unlabelled inputs, at strength alpha = 0.75.
- **Position bias.** Reversing a 20-option list changes about 33% of answers. AnyJev reads all K cyclic rotations and averages log-probabilities; if a logit splits as content + position, this cancels the position term exactly (a probability average would not).
- **Fewer rotations.** K rotations cost K prefills. Reading stops once the log-odds gap between the top two options passes a threshold chosen without labels, so the guarantee is only agreement with the full-rotation decision on at least 1 - epsilon of inputs (Clopper-Pearson bound), not correctness.
- **Confidence.** Calibration needs a temperature fitted on 100 to 500 labelled examples per question.

## Evaluated on
- Rotations cut order dependence on every model: order-flip rate 0.328 to 0.138 (banking, 20 options) and 0.334 to 0.183 (newsgroups), with accuracy up on 11 of 11 models on both, by 7.2 and 5.9 points on the means.
- Calibration comes from the temperature, not the corrections: ECE 0.251 to 0.223 after rotations + prior, then 0.088 with temperature.
- No accuracy gain where options are few: on the JevBench public subset (3.6 options on average), pooled change is +0.006 with an interval spanning zero.
- On 46,320 BEV Decision Mix decisions, the best AnyJev (rotations, prior off) reaches 68.01% vs 70.57% for the fine-tuned Bespoke-Nimble-9B, with lower ECE (0.0490 vs 0.0889). Adding the label prior lowers accuracy to 65.01%.
- Early stopping, selected and verified on separate splits, reads 10.6 of 18 rotations at a 0.008 bound, but certifies only 2 of 4 cells. The serving run (selected and bounded on the same split) reads 7.3 and serves 2.2 times as many decisions per second on vLLM.

## Limitations admitted by the authors
- One prefill cannot reason: on the hardest JevBench tier the eight models score 0.343 to 0.590, so hard decisions stay out of reach.
- Rotations do not beat the best single ordering. The average lands 0.3 to 2.3 points below the best single rotation; it protects against a bad ordering rather than adding accuracy.
- The label prior's benefit depends on the task. It helps on the 20-option sweep but lowers accuracy on the full BEV split, and it assumes the label distribution of the inputs is not extreme.
- The serving-run threshold was selected and bounded on the same sample: the held-out disagreement of 0.013 has an interval [0.004, 0.034] that contains the 1% target, so the run neither confirms nor refutes the guarantee.
- The 20-option results have no per-item intervals, only a sign test over 11 models, on the authors' own tasks.
- Two labelled quantities remain (alpha and the temperature), so "label-free" is true only of the rotation average.

## What it leaves untried
- Canonical (sorted) option order before rotating: proposed, never measured, and the obvious fix for the residual 0.14 to 0.18 flip rate.
- A label-free way to detect miscalibration, or a per-item calibration. One global temperature made Granite worse (ECE 0.270 to 0.301) because its confidence is wrong in different directions on different items.
- Using the calibrated probability as an accept-or-escalate signal for a judging cascade, and checking whether its errors are correlated with the fallback model's (named as a use case, never tested).
