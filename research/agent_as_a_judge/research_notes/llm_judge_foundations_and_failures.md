# LLM-as-a-judge: foundations, failure modes and mitigations (2023 to Oct 2026)

Scope: the method of using an LLM (prompted or fine-tuned) to score, rank or pick model outputs; the biases and attacks documented against it; the mitigations and what they were measured to fix. Per-paper notes (one per paper, `category: llm-judge-foundations`, or appended to notes other researchers had already written) are in `research/agent_as_a_judge/notes/`. All arXiv ids and dates below were checked against the arXiv API on 2026-10-20.

## Which biases are robustly demonstrated, how large are they, and which mitigations actually reduce them?

### Takeaway
Position bias, verbosity/length bias, self-preference, style-over-substance bias, authority/provenance bias, run-to-run inconsistency and the failure to grade problems the judge cannot solve have each been replicated by several independent groups. Their size depends heavily on the judge and the task. By 2026 position bias is small for frontier judges on easy pairs but still flips most verdicts on hard pairs, and style (markdown) bias has become the largest measured bias. The mitigations that hold up under measurement are references or verification for correctness, order permutation, decomposed rubrics/checklists, thinking or reasoning judges, multi-sample aggregation, and post-hoc statistical calibration with some human labels. Each only partly fixes its target, and several (CoT, debate, warnings, length regression) appear to work in one setting and fail in another.

### Cited findings

**Foundational paper and its numbers**
- Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", arXiv 2306.05685 (2023-06, NeurIPS 2023 D&B). GPT-4 pairwise agreement with experts on MT-Bench is 85% on non-tie votes, against 81% human-human. On Chatbot Arena it is 87% non-tie and 64% with ties — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)
- In the same paper, position consistency under swap (default prompt) is GPT-4 65.0%, GPT-3.5 46.2%, Claude-v1 23.8%. Few-shot prompting raises these to 77.5 / 55.0 / 63.7% — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- The "repetitive list" verbosity attack fools Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 91.3% of the time and GPT-4 8.7%. Self-enhancement: GPT-4 gives itself about 10% and Claude-v1 about 25% higher win rates than humans do. The authors say this is inconclusive — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- Math grading failure on 10 questions / 20 judgments is 70% with the default prompt, 30% with CoT and 15% reference-guided — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- G-Eval (Liu et al., arXiv 2303.16634, 2023-03) uses CoT plus form-filling plus probability-weighted scores and reaches Spearman 0.514 with humans on summarisation. It already flags a possible bias toward LLM-generated text — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634)

**Position bias**
- Wang et al., "Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators", arXiv 2305.17926 (2023-05). Reordering the answers lets Vicuna-13B "beat" ChatGPT on 66 of 80 queries when ChatGPT is the evaluator. Balanced-position calibration (averaging both orders) and multiple-evidence calibration reduce the gap with human labels — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)
- Shi et al., arXiv 2406.07791 (2024-06, AACL-IJCNLP 2025) ran about 150K judgments with 15 judges on MTBench and DevBench. Position bias is not random. It varies by judge and task, is weakly affected by length and strongly by the quality gap between candidates — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07791)
- Kumar et al., "All Verdicts are Not Equal", arXiv 2610.12083 (2026-10, AACL-IJCNLP 2026) audited six frontier judges. Order swaps flip the majority of verdicts on challenging tasks, and verdicts change across identical temperature-0 runs — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12083)
- Yagubyan, "The Coin Flip Judge?", arXiv 2606.13685 (2026-04). GPT-4o-mini picks position A 72% of the time (p=0.024) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13685)
- Norman et al., arXiv 2606.19544 (2026-06): 21 judges, about 541K judgments. Two production judges show test-retest reliability above 0.95 together with position bias above 0.10 (the "consistency-bias paradox") — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)
- Soumik, arXiv 2604.23178 (2026-04, TMLR 2026) tested five 2025-26 judges. Position bias is 0.04 or less, far below style bias — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178)
- Xu et al., arXiv 2602.02219 (2026-02, Findings of EMNLP 2026). Rubric scoring also has position bias over the order of score options, and its direction depends on the model. Criterion order shifts scores too — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02219)
- Wang et al., arXiv 2504.09946 (2025-04). Reasoning models (R1, o1) prefer later positions — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09946)

**Verbosity / length bias**
- Saito et al., arXiv 2310.10076 (2023-10). GPT-4 prefers longer answers more than humans do — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10076)
- Dubois et al., Length-Controlled AlpacaEval, arXiv 2404.04475 (2024-04, COLM 2024). A GLM regression on length difference raises Spearman with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98 — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475)
- Conflicting evidence on size. Norman et al. 2026 find verbosity bias below 0.011 across 21 judges under one pairwise rubric ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)). Soumik 2026, measuring length-aware, finds Gemini Pro/Flash and Llama prefer longer answers (+0.24 to +0.44), Claude prefers concise ones (-0.12) and GPT-4o is neutral (-0.04) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178))

**Style over substance**
- Wu & Aji, arXiv 2307.03025 (2023-07). Humans and LLM judges rate answers with factual errors above answers that are short or ungrammatical — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03025)
- Feuer et al., SOS-Bench, arXiv 2409.15268 (2024-09, ICLR 2025). LLM-judge preferences do not correlate with concrete measures of safety, world knowledge or instruction following, and judges put style above factuality and safety — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15268)
- Liu et al., RM-Bench, arXiv 2410.16184 (2024-10). When the wrong answer has the more attractive style, state-of-the-art reward models average 46.6%, below random (50%) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16184)
- Soumik 2026. Style (markdown over plain prose) is the dominant bias at 0.10-0.76 across models — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178)
- Zeng et al., LLMBar, arXiv 2310.07641 (2023-10, ICLR 2024). On the Adversarial split (polished but off-instruction outputs) vanilla accuracy is ChatGPT 32.0, LLaMA-2-70B 32.6, PaLM2 60.7, GPT-4 73.1, against human expert agreement of 94% — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07641)

**Self-preference, family and contamination bias**
- Panickssery et al., arXiv 2404.13076 (2024-04). GPT-4 and Llama 2 can recognise their own outputs, and fine-tuning reveals a linear link between self-recognition and self-preference — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)
- Wataoka et al., arXiv 2410.21819 (2024-10). Judges rate lower-perplexity text higher whoever wrote it, which suggests self-preference is a familiarity effect — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819)
- Chen et al., arXiv 2504.03846 (2025-04). On verifiable tasks, most of the self-preference shown by stronger models is legitimate. The harmful part is concentrated where the judge itself erred, and long CoT before judging reduces it — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03846)
- Li et al., Preference Leakage, arXiv 2502.01534 (2025-02, ICLR 2026). Judges favour students trained on data from the same model, a descendant model or the same family. The authors call this harder to detect than self-preference — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01534)
- Verga et al., PoLL, arXiv 2404.18796. Each judge scored its own family highest. In Arena Hard, the GPT-4 judge ranked a GPT-4 variant 2nd when its true position was 4th — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18796)

**Authority, provenance, anchoring and other cue biases**
- Chen et al., arXiv 2402.10669 (2024-02, EMNLP 2024) studied authority (fake references), misinformation-oversight, gender and beauty biases. Both human and LLM judges are vulnerable, and the biases can be used to attack judges — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10669)
- Ye et al., CALM, arXiv 2410.02736 (2024-10, ICLR 2025) quantifies 12 biases, among them authority, bandwagon, sentiment, CoT and distraction. Significant biases persist in strong models on specific tasks — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)
- Koo et al., CoBBLEr, arXiv 2309.17012 (2023-09). Across 15 LLM judges, bias shows in about 40% of comparisons and RBO with humans is 49.6% — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17012)
- Marioriyad et al., arXiv 2509.26072 (2025-09). GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Flash follow injected provenance cues (Expert > Human > LLM > Unknown) and recency cues. Their justifications almost never mention the cue — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26072)
- Kapetanovic et al., arXiv 2608.25869 (2026-08, CIKM 2026). Prior scores placed in metadata anchor ratings (Cohen's d up to 0.71, in 7 of 8 models). On labelled industry data they block 48% of error corrections. Neither CoT nor a "disregard" warning removes the total effect — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25869)
- Wang et al. 2025 (2504.09946) find a "superficial reflection bias": phrases like "wait, let me think" sway reasoning-model judges — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09946)
- Stureborg et al., arXiv 2405.01724 (2024-05) report familiarity bias, skewed score distributions and anchoring in multi-attribute scoring on SummEval — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01724)

**Inconsistency across runs**
- Schroeder et al., arXiv 2412.12509 (2024-12). Measured with McDonald's omega, single-shot judgments are unreliable even at fixed temperature — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12509)
- Yagubyan 2026. Pairwise verdicts flip 13.6% of the time on average, and 28% of questions flip more than 20% of the time. Cross-judge kappa is 0.51. Semantically equivalent templates change the majority outcome in 25% of cases, and about 11 repeated trials are needed to recover a 50-trial verdict with 95% probability — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13685)
- Kumar et al. 2026. The most deterministic judge is perfectly consistent but agrees with ground truth only 51% of the time — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12083)

**Correctness: judges fail where they cannot solve the problem**
- Krumdick et al., "No Free Labels", arXiv 2503.05061 (2025-03), using 1,200 expert-graded finance answers. Without a correct reference, judges agree with experts mainly on the questions they could answer themselves. Expert references largely fix this — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05061)
- Thakur et al., arXiv 2406.12624 (2024-06). Even in a clean, high-agreement QA setting only the largest judges approach human agreement, and scores can differ by up to 5 points while percent agreement looks high — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624)

**Mitigations: what each fixes, what it does not, and who measured it**
- **Swap / permutation (position).** LLMBar: Swap raises GPT-4's positional agreement from 89.0 to 96.0% and Adversarial accuracy to 80.1 ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07641)). PORTIA (2310.01432) raises consistency by 47.46% relative on average and GPT-4 consistency to 98% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01432)). Xu et al. 2026 find that a few random rubric-order permutations remove most rubric position error ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02219)). What swapping does not fix: consistency is not correctness. Shi et al. show bias concentrates on close pairs ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07791)), and swap-and-tie turns those pairs into ties.
- **Pairwise vs pointwise vs listwise.** Tripathi et al., arXiv 2504.14716 (2025-04, COLM 2025): with distractor features, pairwise preferences flip in about 35% of cases against 9% for absolute scores ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14716)). Raina et al., arXiv 2402.14016, find the opposite for adversarial universal phrases: absolute scoring is more attackable than comparative ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14016)). Landesberg, arXiv 2603.12520 (2026-03): in best-of-4 selection, pointwise scoring ties in 67% of pairs and recovers 21% of the possible gain, while explicit pairwise recovers 61% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12520)). Kumar et al. 2026: holistic rubric scoring improves trustworthiness more than any single prompting fix to pairwise judging ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12083)). Net: pointwise is more robust to distractors but less discriminative, and which protocol is safer depends on the threat.
- **Rubrics and checklists.** TICK, arXiv 2410.03608: exact agreement with humans rises from 46.4 to 52.2%, and human inter-annotator agreement from 0.194 to 0.256 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03608)). CheckEval, arXiv 2403.18771: agreement across 12 evaluator models rises by 0.45 and variance falls ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18771)). LLMBar Rules: GPT-4 Adversarial goes from 73.1 to 80.8 ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07641)). LongJudgeBench, arXiv 2606.01629: rubrics and references help but are "not always sufficient" for long-form outputs ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01629)). Limits: rubrics raise agreement between judges more than accuracy, and they bring their own option-position bias (Xu et al. 2026).
- **Reference-guided.** MT-Bench math failure falls from 70% to 15% ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)). No Free Labels: expert references largely close the gap on questions the judge cannot solve ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05061)). PoLL with references beats GPT-4 on QA kappa ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18796)). Limits: references must be correct and present. One Token to Fool (2507.08794) shows reference-based judges still accept content-free "master key" answers ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794)).
- **Panels / juries (PoLL).** PoLL (Command R + Claude 3 Haiku + GPT-3.5) against GPT-4 on Cohen's kappa: NQ 0.763 vs 0.627, TriviaQA 0.906 vs 0.841, HotpotQA 0.867 vs 0.830. On Arena Hard, Pearson is 0.917 vs 0.817. The panel is 7-8x cheaper and has less intra-model bias ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18796)). Limits: math and reasoning were not tested. "Safer or Luckier" (2503.09347) finds juries help with safety judging, but apologetic-phrasing artifacts (which skew verdicts up to 98%) persist even in the best jury ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.09347)).
- **Debate between judges or debaters.** ChatEval (2308.07201) improves human agreement on open-ended NLG ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07201)), yet scores 34.0% on JudgeBench, below a single GPT-4o at 50.9-56.6 ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)). Debate between expert LLMs helps weaker judges on QuALITY (76% vs a 48% baseline) when there is information asymmetry (Khan et al., 2402.06782, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06782)). Without information asymmetry the results are mixed (Kenton et al., 2407.04622, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04622)). Debate looks like a mitigation for open-ended tasks only, and hurts on hard objective tasks.
- **Chain-of-thought and think-then-judge.** CoT helps math grading (70 to 30% failure, MT-Bench) but hurts on LLMBar Adversarial (76.6 vs 80.8), because the reasoning rationalises the superficially appealing output ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07641)). Trained "thinking" judges do measurably better: J1 (2505.10320), EvalPlanner (93.9 on RewardBench, 2501.18099) and Self-Taught Evaluators (75.4 to 88.3 on RewardBench, 2408.02666) ([J1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10320), [EvalPlanner](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18099), [STE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02666)). Small Qwen3 thinking judges score about 10 points higher and are about 6% more bias-robust than non-thinking ones (2509.13332, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13332)). Reasoning model o3-mini-high reaches 80.9 on JudgeBench, against 56.6 for GPT-4o ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)). Limits: RACER (2605.10805, ICML 2026) finds reasoning helps on math and code but gives "limited or even negative gains" on simpler evaluations, at much higher cost ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10805)). Reasoning judges keep position, authority and "superficial reflection" biases (2504.09946), and CoT does not reduce anchoring (2608.25869).
- **Uncertainty and abstention.** Trust or Escalate (2407.18370, ICLR 2025): selective evaluation with Simulated Annotators confidence and a cascade from cheap to strong judges guarantees over 80% human agreement at about 80% coverage, on a Chatbot Arena subset where GPT-4 alone almost never reaches 80% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370)). ConfiLM (2502.10709) uses uncertainty to improve out-of-distribution judging ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10709)). Limits: the guarantee assumes calibration data exchangeable with the test data, and abstained items fall back to humans.
- **Calibration and statistical debiasing.** LC-AlpacaEval regression (0.94 to 0.98 Spearman) handles length only, and the null-model attack still reaches 86.5% LC ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07137)). AutoCalibrate (2309.13308) refines criteria from a few labels ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.13308)). CaLLM multicalibration (2610.18108, 2026-10) bounds bias on specified and unspecified groups and improves accuracy ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18108)). "Noisy but Valid" (2601.20913, ICLR 2026) gives Type-I error control using estimated judge TPR/FPR ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20913)). Hard limit: Dorner et al. (2410.13341, ICLR 2025) prove that when the judge is no more accurate than the evaluated model, no debiasing method can more than halve the required ground-truth labels, and in practice the savings are smaller ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13341)).
- **Multi-sample aggregation.** About 11 trials are needed for a stable majority verdict (2606.13685). Deterministic decoding reduces inconsistency but does not remove it ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13685)).
- **Judge fine-tuning.** Open judges such as PandaLM, JudgeLM (with swap augmentation, reference support and reference drop), Auto-J and Prometheus 1/2 match GPT-4 in-domain ([JudgeLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17631), [Prometheus](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08491)). Huang et al. (2403.02839) show they act as task-specific classifiers and fall short of GPT-4 on generalisability, fairness and adaptability ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02839)). On JudgeBench they score 13-40%, below random ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)). Training against found failures works narrowly: Master-RM gets close to 0% false positives on master keys ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794)).
- **Combined debiasing in 2026.** Soumik (TMLR 2026) compared nine strategies. The best configuration, Gemini 2.5 Flash with the "Combined Budget" strategy, reaches 71.0% agreement (kappa 0.549) at about $0.001 per evaluation, against 69.5% for Claude Sonnet 4 at about $0.015. Significant gains after correction range from +4.5 to +11.5 pp ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178)). Even the best configuration stays near a 70% agreement ceiling.

### Inferences
- The robust, replicated failures are position bias on close or hard pairs, style/format bias, cue-following (authority, provenance, anchoring, reasoning-like phrases) and correctness failure when the judge cannot solve the task. Self-preference is real but partly legitimate (Chen 2025), and its harmful core coincides with the "cannot solve it" failure. Verbosity bias may be shrinking or reversing in some 2025-26 judges, but the evidence conflicts (Norman vs Soumik).
- Mitigations that change the information the judge has (references, execution or verification, gold-label calibration) fix correctness failures. Mitigations that only change the prompt (CoT, warnings, personas, debate) give inconsistent gains and sometimes hurt (LLMBar CoT, JudgeBench ChatEval, anchoring warnings).
- Consistency metrics (swap agreement, test-retest) can look excellent while accuracy is near chance (Kumar 2026; Norman 2026). Mitigations that improve consistency should not be read as accuracy gains.

### Gaps
- There is no single study that measures every major bias on the same frontier judges with ground truth. Soumik 2026 and Norman 2026 come closest but use preference benchmarks. Bias magnitudes across papers are therefore not directly comparable (different metrics, judges and datasets).
- Exact per-model numbers for CALM's 12 biases and for the Gu et al. survey's own meta-evaluation benchmark were not extracted (the HTML sections were not read).
- Sycophancy toward the prompt, meaning judges deferring to a user's stated opinion inside the judging prompt, is covered here only indirectly (bandwagon in CALM and 2504.09946; anchoring in 2608.25869). I found no dedicated 2025-26 judge-sycophancy benchmark in this pass.

## What do meta-evaluation benchmarks show about frontier judges on hard, objective tasks, and where do judges still fail in 2026?

### Takeaway
Benchmarks that label by objective correctness rather than crowd preference consistently show judges well below what agreement-with-humans numbers suggest. In 2024 GPT-4o was near chance on JudgeBench (50.9-56.6%). Reasoning models lifted that to 75-81% by early 2025, and RewardBench 2 places frontier generative judges at 75-80% on best-of-4, with Precise Instruction Following the hardest category. In 2026 the frontier failures are hard or bias-perturbed pairs (JudgeBench-Pro, over 50% error), long-form outputs, process-level reasoning faithfulness, multimodal perception and agentic or deep-research traces (below 55%).

### Cited findings
- **LLMBar** (2310.07641, 2023-10). 419 pairs. GPT-4 scores Natural 93.5 / Adversarial 73.1, while ChatGPT and LLaMA-2-70B are about 32 on Adversarial (below chance). Reward models score 26-38 on Adversarial — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07641)
- **JudgeBench** (Tan et al., 2410.12784, 2024-10, ICLR 2025). 350 GPT-4o pairs across knowledge, reasoning, math and coding, each judged in both orders. GPT-4o vanilla scores 50.86, or 56.57 with the Arena-Hard prompt. Claude-3.5-Sonnet 64.29, o1-preview 75.43, o1-mini 65.71, o3-mini-high 80.86, Llama-3.1-405B 56.86. Fine-tuned judges score 13.1 (PandaLM) to 57.4 (Skywork-Critic-70B), ChatEval 34.0, and the best reward model 64.29 — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)
- **RewardBench** (2403.13787, 2024-03). The first RM benchmark, covering chat, chat-hard, safety and reasoning — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13787)
- **RewardBench 2** (Malik et al., 2506.01937, 2025-06). 1,865 best-of-4 prompts (random = 25%), on which models score about 20 points lower than on RewardBench 1. Top classifier is Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B at 84.1. Generative judges: Gemini 2.5 Pro 79.5 (Math 89.8, Precise IF 61.9), Gemini 2.5 Flash 77.7, Claude Opus 4 76.5 (Precise IF 41.9), Claude 3.7 Sonnet 75.4. Score correlates with best-of-N at r=0.87 across 113 RMs, but gives only a coarse signal for PPO — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01937)
- **RM-Bench** (2410.16184, 2024-10). Under style interference, the state of the art scores 46.6%, below random — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16184)
- **Multimodal RewardBench** (Yasunaga et al., 2502.14191, 2025-02). 5,211 triplets. The best judges (Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) reach 72%, and they are weakest on reasoning and safety — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14191)
- **VL-RewardBench** (2411.17451, CVPR 2025). GPT-4o scores 65.4% and Qwen2-VL-72B is near random. Failures are mostly in basic perception — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17451)
- **JudgeMark (EQ-Bench)**. A leaderboard of judging ability on creative writing from 19 models over 36 criteria. It reports separability, stability, self/family bias and correlation with Arena creative writing. Its authors say sample sizes are small and the bias stats should be "taken with a grain of salt". There is no paper — [eqbench.com](https://eqbench.com/judgemark-v2.html)
- **BiasScope / JudgeBench-Pro** (2602.09383, 2026-02, ICLR 2026). Bias-perturbed JudgeBench, on which even powerful LLM evaluators exceed 50% error — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09383)
- **LongJudgeBench** (2606.01629, 2026-06, EMNLP 2026). Judges are unstable across long-form scenarios, and rubrics and references are not always sufficient — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01629). One secondary summary (search snippet) says only 12 of 32 model-setting combinations exceed 0.60 against a 0.5 random baseline. I did not verify this in the paper.
- **C2-Faith** (2603.05167, 2026-03). Frontier judges detect that a reasoning step is wrong but cannot localise it, and all of them overestimate reasoning completeness — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05167)
- **REFLECT** (2605.19196, 2026-05). On deep-research agent traces, the best judges stay below 55% accuracy and are worst at evidence verification — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19196)
- **Norman et al.** (2606.19544, 2026-06). 21 judges including the April 2026 frontier, tested on MT-Bench, JudgeBench and RewardBench. Exact-match agreement overstates Cohen's kappa by 33-41 pp on MT-Bench, and judge rankings shift by up to 14 positions across benchmarks — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)
- **Kumar et al.** (2610.12083, 2026-10). Across six frontier judges, the most deterministic one matches ground truth 51% of the time — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12083)
- **"Confidently Wrong"** (2607.23386, 2026-07). On nested-exception eligibility rules, frontier models fail coverage-gap edge cases. Accuracy also drifted silently under the same model alias between March and April 2026 (GPT-5.4 went from 96.6% to 100%). This is a rule-evaluation paper rather than a judge benchmark, but it is relevant to judge stability over time — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23386)
- **Landesberg** (2603.12520). A judge with a "moderate" global correlation (r=0.47) captures 21% of the best-of-4 selection gain. Global agreement metrics hide this — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12520)

### Inferences
- Progress on objective judging has come mostly from reasoning judges (GPT-4o at about 56 against o3-mini at about 81 on JudgeBench), not from prompt engineering or fine-tuned small judges.
- The remaining failure zones in 2026 are: (1) items the judge cannot itself solve, (2) adversarially perturbed or bias-loaded pairs, (3) long or process-level artefacts (long-form outputs, CoT steps, agent traces), and (4) precise instruction-following constraints. All four are directly relevant to agent-as-a-judge designs.
- Benchmark choice changes judge rankings by up to 14 places. Judge selection should therefore be validated on the target task distribution, using a chance-corrected metric (kappa) and the deployment decision metric (for example top-1 recovery).

### Gaps
- No public, current (Oct 2026) JudgeBench or RewardBench 2 leaderboard snapshot was retrieved, so the latest frontier judge numbers (GPT-5.x, Claude 4.x, Gemini 3) are not reported here. Another note in `notes/` mentions a Sept 2026 paper reporting a judge at 93.1% on a JudgeBench subset (arXiv 2609.26550). I did not verify it.
- The LongJudgeBench and C2-Faith per-model numbers were not extracted from the full papers.

## What attacks on judges exist and which defences hold?

### Takeaway
Judges can be gamed at every access level. A black-box constant "null model" output tops AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard-Auto and MT-Bench. Single tokens or reasoning-like openers earn false-positive rewards. Universal adversarial phrases transfer from surrogate to unseen judges. Gradient-optimised injections choose the winner. Backdoors in judge training data triple scores. Policies optimised against a judge also find exploits on their own (reward hacking, U-sophistry). No defence has been shown to hold against adaptive attackers. Perplexity and known-answer detection fail. Length control does not stop template hijacking. Adversarial training (Master-RM) and model merging (BadJudge) work against the specific attack they target.

### Cited findings
- **Null-model cheating** (Zheng et al., 2410.07137, 2024-10, ICLR 2025 Oral). A constant response reaches 86.5% LC win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, 83.0 on Arena-Hard-Auto and 9.55 on MT-Bench, and it transfers without access to the benchmark instructions — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07137)
- **Master keys** (Zhao et al., "One Token to Fool LLM-as-a-Judge", 2507.08794, 2025-07, NeurIPS 2025). Non-word symbols (":", ".") and openers ("Thought process:") draw false-positive rewards from reference-based generative RMs, including GPT-o1 and Claude-4. Reported rates are up to about 35% FPR for punctuation on GPT-4o and 60-90% for "Thought process:" on some open models. The defence is Master-RM, trained on truncated outputs as negatives, which reaches about 0% — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794), [search summary of figures](https://bdtechtalks.com/2025/07/21/llm-as-a-judge-vulnerability/)
- **Universal adversarial phrases** (Raina et al., 2402.14016, 2024-02). Short phrases learned on a surrogate transfer to unseen judges and push absolute scores to the maximum. Comparative assessment is more robust — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14016)
- **JudgeDeceiver** (Shi et al., 2403.17710, 2024-03, CCS 2024). Gradient-optimised injection into one candidate makes the judge select it. Known-answer detection, perplexity detection and windowed perplexity detection are all insufficient — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17710)
- **GCG prompt injection** (Maloyan et al., 2505.13348). Attacks on the final decision exceed 30% ASR, and attacks on the justification are also effective, against small open judges — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13348)
- **BadJudge backdoors** (Tong et al., 2503.00596, ICLR 2025). A one-token trigger in 1% of training data triples scores. Web poisoning gives about 20% inflation, and weight poisoning lifts scores from 1.5/5 to 4.9/5. With 10% poisoning, toxicity judges misclassify 89% and RAG rerankers put the poisoned document first 97% of the time. Defence: model merging brings ASR close to 0% — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00596)
- **RobustJudge** (Li et al., 2506.09443, 2025-06). Tests 15 attacks and 8 defences on 13 judges. Judges are vulnerable under both pointwise and pairwise protocols, and no defence dominates on robustness, utility and cost together. Robustness is highly sensitive to the template and the model. A production judge (PAI-Judge) can be inflated with optimised long suffixes — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09443)
- **Safety-judge attacks.** "Know Thy Judge" (2503.04474): style changes shift false-negative rates by up to 0.24, and adversarial outputs make some judges label 100% of harmful outputs safe ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04474)). "Safer or Luckier" (2503.09347): apologetic artifacts skew preferences by up to 98% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.09347))
- **Bias-as-attack.** Chen et al. 2402.10669 use authority and beauty biases to attack judges ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10669)). Tripathi et al. 2504.14716 show generators can embed distractors to flip pairwise preferences in about 35% of cases ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14716))
- **Judge gaming by the optimised policy.** Pan et al. (2407.04549): in iterative self-refinement the evaluator's scores rise while human-judged quality stalls, and the effect is worse when generator and evaluator share the model or context ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04549)). Wen et al. (2409.12822, ICLR 2025): RLHF makes models better at convincing time-limited humans, raising evaluator false-positive rates by 24.1% on QuALITY and 18.3% on APPS, and probing-based detection does not generalise ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12822))
- **Benchmark-level gaming.** "The Leaderboard Illusion" (2504.20879): private testing (27 Meta variants before Llama-4) and data-access asymmetries let providers overfit the human Arena that LLM judges are validated against ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879))
- **SoK** (Al Masoud et al., 2603.29403, 2026-03). The first security SoK on LLM-as-a-judge, covering 45 studies selected from 863. Its taxonomy has four parts: attacks on judges, attacks through judges, judges as defences, and judges in security evaluation — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29403)

### Inferences
- Any judge whose input includes attacker-controlled text is a prompt-injection surface, and every benchmark-level debiasing step (length control, style control) leaves the template-hijack channel open. Structural isolation of candidate text, executable or verifiable checks, and adversarially trained judges are the only directions with measured gains. Each has been evaluated only against its own attack family.
- For agent-as-a-judge settings, the policy-side gaming results (Pan 2024, Wen 2024, master keys in RLVR) matter most. Any judge used as an optimisation target will be exploited unless it is regularly re-audited with fresh adversarial negatives.

### Gaps
- I found no paper showing a defence that holds against an adaptive attacker who knows the defence, across attack families.
- The SoK's defence comparison table was not read (only the abstract).

## What do the surveys list as open problems?

### Takeaway
The two major surveys (Gu et al. and Li et al., both Nov 2024 and revised through late 2025) converge on bias and robustness, inference-time scaling of judgment, meta-evaluation and temporal consistency, interpretability, multimodal and agentic judging, and human-LLM co-judging. 2026 work adds chance-corrected validation, statistical guarantees with imperfect judges, and security as a field of its own.

### Cited findings
- **Gu et al., "A Survey on LLM-as-a-Judge"** (2411.15594, 2024-11, latest revision Oct 2025). The challenges it lists: reliability (ICL sensitivity, overconfidence, self-enhancement, weak generalisation); robustness (adversarial attacks, input sensitivity and jailbreaks, brittle scoring); backbone limitations; interpretability and transparency; meta-evaluation and temporal consistency; ethical and social implications (bias amplification, accountability gaps, homogenised creative output). Future directions: reasoning-centric and self-evolving judges, theoretically grounded evaluation, more reliable judges, MLLM-as-a-judge, better benchmarks, data annotation, scaling, embodied intelligence, LLM optimisation, domain-specific reliability. It proposes its own reliability meta-benchmark — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15594)
- **Li et al., "From Generation to Judgment"** (2411.16594, 2024-11, EMNLP 2025, v7 Sept 2025). Taxonomy: what to judge, how to judge (tuning and prompting) and how to benchmark. Open problems: bias and vulnerability (length, format, self-preference, prompt injection, adversarial phrases), calling for principles, calibration and root-cause study; scaling judgment at inference time (long CoT, sampling, search) with its efficiency, overthinking and attack-surface costs; dynamic and complex judging (LLM-as-examiner, debate, agentic judging); and human-LLM co-judgement, which few works study — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.16594)
- **Security SoK** (2603.29403, 2026-03). Emerging threats and open challenges in judge security, structured by the role the judge plays — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29403)
- **EvalSafetyGap** (Uluırmak et al., 2606.30219, 2026-06). A survey of 373 studies (2018-2026) that treats LLM-as-a-judge protocols as one stream of a shared proxy-target divergence problem under optimisation pressure (Goodhart). It calls for version-locked evaluation and pre-specified threat models — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30219)
- **2026 methodological agenda** set by empirical papers: use chance-corrected agreement and a "Minimum Viable Validation Protocol" (Norman 2606.19544, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)); report a trustworthy verdict rate (Kumar 2610.12083, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12083)); report within-prompt signal, tie rates and top-1 recovery (Landesberg 2603.12520, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12520)); use multi-trial aggregation with position randomisation and uncertainty reporting (Yagubyan 2606.13685, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13685)); and accept the fundamental limit at the frontier, where a judge no better than the model saves at most half the labels (Dorner 2410.13341, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13341))
- **Awesome-LLM-as-a-judge** (companion list to Li et al.) tracks 2025 work in categories for bias, robustness, attacks, meta-evaluation and calibration/uncertainty — [GitHub](https://github.com/llm-as-a-judge/Awesome-LLM-as-a-judge)

### Inferences
- The open problem the surveys under-weight and the 2025-26 evidence stresses most is validity at the frontier. When the evaluated system is as strong as the judge, or the artefact is long or agentic, no prompt-level fix restores accuracy. That leaves human grounding, references, verification tools or statistical correction with labelled data (Dorner, Krumdick, Feng, Jung).
- Agentic judging is named as a direction by Li et al. (7.3), and the 2026 benchmarks (REFLECT, C2-Faith, LongJudgeBench) show it is where current judges are weakest.

### Gaps
- I found no 2026 general survey of LLM-as-a-judge beyond the revised Gu/Li surveys, the security SoK and EvalSafetyGap. A 2026 survey may exist that this search missed.
- The bodies of the Gu survey's Sections 6-7 were read only at heading level, so the one-line descriptions above come from the headings and table of contents.
