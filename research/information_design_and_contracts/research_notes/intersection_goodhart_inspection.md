# Information design meets contract theory: persuasion under moral hazard, inspection games, Goodhart formalisations, hidden and moving evaluation criteria, subjective evaluation

Scope note: economics / game theory / CS-theory only. "Classic" = pre-2020; "recent" = 2022-2026 flagged inline. Every claim carries the source it was read from; where I only saw an abstract or a secondary citation, the bullet says so.

## 1. Formal links between information design and contracts: the unified view, and persuasion inside a principal-agent problem

### Takeaway
Information design (Bergemann-Morris) and mechanism design share one skeleton: Myerson's 1986 communication equilibrium already treats "what the mediator tells each player" and "what each player is asked to do" as one object, with an obedience constraint. Putting a hidden-effort agent inside a persuasion problem (Boleslavsky-Kim) adds an incentive-compatibility constraint that can be written as an expectation over posteriors, so the signal is chosen by concavifying objective and IC constraint together; the main lessons are that full revelation maximises feasible effort in binary-state models, that fully uninformative signals are never optimal once effort matters, that optimal signals are coarse (two or three messages), and that coarsening *can* raise effort when fine signals would punish states the agent's effort makes more likely.

### Cited Findings
- Bergemann & Morris, "Information Design: A Unified Perspective", *Journal of Economic Literature* 57(1), 44-95, March 2019 (Cowles DP 2075R / CEPR DP 11867): information design "identifies the information structure and equilibrium that maximizes the payoff of an information designer for a game with uncertain payoffs"; it unifies "communication in games (Myerson 1991)" and "Bayesian persuasion (Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011)"; the authors distinguish a literal reading (a real designer commits to an information structure) from a metaphorical one (the analyst characterises play across all information structures). — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cwl/cwldpp/2075r.html)
- Myerson, "Multistage Games with Communication", *Econometrica* 54(2), 323-358, March 1986: communication mechanisms implemented by a central mediator; a *communication equilibrium* is one in which "no player expects ex ante to gain by manipulating his reports or actions"; the sequential version is characterised by never using *codominated* actions, and predominant communication equilibria exist. — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1986/03/01/multistage-games-communication)
- Forges, "An Approach to Communication Equilibria", *Econometrica* 54(6), 1375-1385, Nov 1986, introduces the extensive-form correlated equilibrium and the communication-equilibrium concept for multistage games (companion to Myerson). — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1986/11/01/approach-communication-equilibria)
- Kamenica & Gentzkow, "Bayesian Persuasion", *AER* 101(6), 2590-2615, 2011, is the single-sender, no-effort benchmark that the papers below extend. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v101y2011i6p2590-2615.html)
- **Boleslavsky & Kim, "Bayesian Persuasion and Moral Hazard"** (working paper, March 2018 NBER conference version; seminar Oct 2017; cited elsewhere as 2021, SSRN 2913669). Three players: sender designs a signal about an unknown state; the agent's private effort determines the state distribution; the receiver acts on the signal. "The signal structure influences the receiver's beliefs through two distinct channels: it determines the prior belief by incentivizing the agent's effort, and it affects the posterior belief by generating information about the realized state." — [NBER conference PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf); [Kellogg seminar listing](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/events/2017/10/25/ralph-boleslavsky); cited as "Boleslavsky and Kim (2021)" in [arXiv 2604.10006](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10006)
  - Worked example (school/student/firm): without moral hazard the KG-optimal grading policy is π(g|s)=1, π(g|u)=3/7 (placement prob 3/5); with hidden effort (work cost c=1/5) the IC constraint binds harder, giving π(g|u)=1/3, placement 8/15, i.e. "in order to eliminate shirking, the school inflates grades less". The firm is strictly better off under moral hazard (posterior 9/16 > 1/2 after g). — [PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf)
  - Proposition 3.1: target effort e is implementable iff there is a distribution of posteriors τ with (i) E_τ[μ]=η(e) (Bayes-plausibility) and (ii) E_τ[E_μ[η_e(ω|e)/η(ω|e)]·v_A(μ)] = c′(e) (incentive compatibility) — i.e. the IC constraint is itself an expectation over posteriors, so "we describe how to concavify the sender's objective function and the incentive constraint simultaneously". — [PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf)
  - Proposition 3.2: with N states, an optimal signal needs at most N+1 posteriors; Proposition 3.3 is a Lagrangian/supporting-hyperplane characterisation (v_S(μ)+ψh(μ) ≤ λ0+⟨λ1,μ⟩ with equality on the support). — [PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf)
  - Proposition 4.1 (binary state): the maximum implementable effort ē satisfies c′(ē)=μ̄−μ̲ and "is incentive compatible if and only if the signal is fully informative". Corollary 4.2: fully informative is optimal when v_S is convex; "a fully uninformative signal is never optimal when v_S is concave" (unlike KG, because no information ⇒ zero effort). Proposition 4.3: for e above the incentive-free level, the IC multiplier ψ>0. — [PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf)
  - Proposition 4.4 (concave sender, linear agent): optimal signal is binary and pins the *variance* of posteriors (IC ⇔ Var(μ)=e(1−e)c′(e)); if v_S‴<0 the support is {0, μ_U} (conclusive bad news; interpreted as "censoring bad news" / a limited search for evidence of failure); if v_S‴>0 it is {μ_D, 1} (conclusive good news). — [PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf)
  - Proposition 4.6 (sender payoff a step function at θ): the optimal signal uses *three* posteriors {0, θ, 1} — "three distinct posteriors ... may be necessary in our model, which is never the case in the absence of moral hazard". — [PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf)
  - Transparency results: if the agent's effort is observable, "transparency 'crowds out' the sender's concern for incentive provision" and the sender reverts to KG; in the HARA example "transparency lowers the agent's equilibrium effort if c ∈ (2/3, 1)". — [PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf)
  - Binary-action model, Corollary 5.2: a fully informative signal induces maximal effort iff the lowest "good-news" state coincides with the receiver's acceptance threshold (ω_e = ω_r); if ω_e < ω_r, full information rewards some states the agent's effort makes *more* likely with rejection, which "undermines the agent's incentive", and pooling all states above ω_e strengthens it. Proposition 5.4: as target effort rises, the optimal binary signal "reveal[s] more bad-news states" continuously. — [PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf)
- **Georgiadis & Szentes, "Optimal Monitoring Design", *Econometrica* 88(5), 2075-2107, Sept 2020** (DOI 10.3982/ECTA16475): principal-agent with hidden action; the principal can acquire independent signals about effort at constant marginal cost; "the optimal information-acquisition strategy follows a two-threshold policy", so the contract pays one of two wages — offered as an explanation for single-bonus contracts. — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2020/09/01/optimal-monitoring-design) (abstract only)
- **Hörner & Lambert, "Motivational Ratings", *Review of Economic Studies* 88, 1892-1935, 2021** (TSE WP 20-1134): the effort-maximising rating in a career-concerns model is a linear function of past observations, is not Markov but the sum of two Markov processes, "blends information of different types and vintages", and "raising effort can hurt some, though not all, future ratings". — [TSE](https://www.tse-fr.eu/articles/motivational-ratings) (abstract only)
- **Zapechelnyuk, "Optimal Quality Certification", *AER: Insights* 2(2), 161-176, 2020** (DOI 10.1257/aeri.20190387): certification treated as a moral-hazard/delegation problem; "under standard assumptions, simple certification systems, such as quality assurance rule and pass-fail rule, are optimal". — [St Andrews repository](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/20319)
- Rodina, job-market paper "Information Design and Career Concerns" (Northwestern, 2017 job talk): studies "how information and incentives interact in principal-agent relationships where the agent cares about being seen as high ability"; main result concerns when more precise performance information or more uncertainty about ability strengthens career-concern incentives; the key condition is "how effort changes how informative performance signals are about ability". — [CERGE-EI seminar listing](https://cerge-ei.cz/micro-theory/2017-02-08/david-rodina) (abstract only)
- Zhao, Mezzetti, Renou & Tomala, "Contracting over Persistent Information" (arXiv 2007.05983, 2020-21): dynamic moral hazard where disclosure of the principal's persistent private information is the *only* incentive tool; the optimal contract stops disclosing once the agent's preferred action becomes a static best reply, or discloses until the agent fully learns. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.05983v3)
- Jehiel, "On Transparency in Organizations" (working paper, c. 2014): "full transparency is generically suboptimal when the principal's private information has a higher dimension than the agent's action". — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10066731/1/Jehiel%2C%20AAM%20transparency.pdf)
- (recent, 2026) Chen & Liu, "Dynamic Persuasion with Dead Ends" (arXiv 2601.13686, Jan 2026): principal uses private information about the agent's payoff as an incentive tool in bandit experimentation; optimal policy uses "at most two one-shot disclosures: an early one that makes the agent optimistic and a later one that makes the agent pessimistic"; the Arrow-Pratt coefficient is a sufficient statistic for the disclosure structure. — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2601.13686)
- (recent, 2026) "Moral Hazard in Delegated Bayesian Persuasion" (arXiv 2604.10006, 2026) extends the B-K line; it is the source listing B-K as 2021/SSRN 2913669. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10006) (seen only through its reference list)
- "Optimal Rating Design under Moral Hazard" (arXiv 2008.09529, 2020) is listed on IDEAS as a further entry in this line (authors not verified by me). — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2008.09529.html)
- (recent, 2024, experimental) Du & Shahriar, *Games and Economic Behavior* 2024: delayed disclosure beats immediate and no disclosure only when immediate disclosure is unavailable; when the principal can choose and picks delay, the advantage disappears through reciprocity. — [abstract listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/324008)

### Inferences
- The B-K machinery is the direct formal template for an evaluator that both *scores* (informs a downstream decision) and *motivates* (shapes the builder's effort): the IC constraint is an expectation over posteriors, so the evaluator's choice of how coarse to make its verdict is a one-dimensional trade-off between informativeness and incentive power.
- "When does withholding information increase effort?" has three sourced answers: (a) B-K Corollary 5.2 — when a fine signal would punish states that effort makes more likely (pooling those states raises the marginal return to effort); (b) B-K "transparency" examples — observability of effort can *lower* effort when it removes the sender's reason to design informative signals; (c) Hörner-Lambert — in dynamic ratings, effort can lower some future ratings, so the effort-maximising rating is a deliberately mixed-vintage linear aggregate rather than the most informative one. In binary-state static models, however, full revelation maximises the *feasible* effort (B-K Prop 4.1), so withholding raises effort only by changing *which* states are pooled, not by reducing information per se.
- Georgiadis-Szentes and Zapechelnyuk converge on coarse (two-wage / pass-fail) outcomes from different primitives (costly signal acquisition; delegation), which supports treating a pass/fail verdict with a hidden threshold as the natural object rather than a fine-grained score.

- Georgiadis-Szentes and Hörner-Lambert: only abstracts retrieved; the exact two-threshold statement and the linear-rating formula are not quoted here.
- Boleslavsky-Kim: I read the 2018 working paper; I did not find a journal version, and the 2021 SSRN revision may differ.

## 2. Monitoring and inspection: inspection games, audit games, random crackdowns, Stackelberg security games

### Takeaway
Inspection games are zero-sum or near-zero-sum games in which an inspector with limited inspections faces an inspectee who can violate and may manipulate data; the equilibrium is in mixed strategies because any predictable inspection schedule is exploited, and the inspector's optimal randomisation is given recursively (Dresher/von Stengel). When the inspector can *commit* (inspector leadership / Stackelberg), the inspectee's best response is pure compliance while inspections remain, which is better for the inspector; CS versions (audit games, security games) add punishment levels and multiple resources and compute the committed mixed strategy.

### Cited Findings
- Avenhaus, von Stengel & Zamir, "Inspection Games", ch. 51 in *Handbook of Game Theory with Economic Applications* vol. III (Aumann & Hart eds., Elsevier 2002), pp. 1947-1987: the field grew out of 1960s arms control; applications in arms control, auditing and insurance, environmental surveillance; inspection is a game "because an inspectee can manipulate the data to hide illegal activity"; the chapter solves material accountancy and data verification models, covers sequential inspection games "where limited inspection resources must be allocated strategically", and *inspector leadership*, "where the inspector commits to a strategy in advance". — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/h/eee/gamchp/3-51.html); [HUJI record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/chapter-51-inspection-games/) (abstract only; the IIASA full text was access-blocked)
- Avenhaus & Canty, "Inspector Leadership", a separate Cambridge book chapter (distinct from the Handbook chapter). — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/CBO9780511526510A045/type/BOOK_PART)
- **von Stengel, "Recursive Inspection Games", *Mathematics of Operations Research* 41(3), 935-952, 2016** (arXiv 1412.0129; LSE eprint 68299): "An inspector uses a limited number of inspections over a larger number of time periods to detect a violation"; generalises Dresher's classical sequential game (fixed number of inspections across periods, one violation); key step: "Under reasonable assumptions for the payoffs, the inspector's strategy is independent of the number of successful violations", which licenses a recursive description "even though this normally assumes fully informed players after each stage"; the equilibrium payoff satisfies a recursion solved explicitly "in terms of sums of binomial coefficients"; covers non-zero-sum versions and inspector leadership, where "the inspectee acts legally (rather than mixes as in the simultaneous game) as long as inspections remain". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.0129); [LSE eprint](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/68299)
- **Blocki, Christin, Datta, Procaccia & Sinha, "Audit Games", IJCAI 2013, pp. 41-47**: audit modelled as a game between an enforcer and an adversary, adding a *punishment parameter* to a standard security-game model; efficient algorithms for choosing audit resource allocation and punishment levels; motivated by privacy-law enforcement in hospitals and banks. — [CMU PDF](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/nicolasc/publications/BCDPS-IJCAI13.pdf)
- Blocki et al., "Audit Games with Multiple Defender Resources", AAAI 2015, pp. 791-797 (arXiv 1409.4503): several audit resources, each able to audit only a subset of violations; an FPTAS for the resulting non-convex optimisation. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.4503)
- Stackelberg security games (Tambe group): the defender "acts first by committing to a patrolling or inspection strategy, and the attacker chooses where to attack after observing the defender's choice"; the solution concept is *strong Stackelberg equilibrium*, where the defender randomises optimally assuming the attacker sees the mixed strategy and best-responds; randomisation "avoids the predictability of human schedulers"; deployed as ARMOR (LAX checkpoints/canines) and IRIS (Federal Air Marshals). — [Tambe/An book chapter](https://personal.ntu.edu.sg/boan/papers/Milindchapter.pdf); survey Sinha, Fang, An, Kiekintveld & Tambe, "Stackelberg Security Games: Looking Beyond a Decade of Success", IJCAI 2018 — [IJCAI](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2018/775); Stackelberg-vs-Nash interchangeability/equivalence results — [Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/security-and-game-theory/stackelberg-versus-nash-in-security-games-interchangeability-equivalence-and-uniqueness/465685D3F3D3AD55CACCBF72ABA1BCD9)
- **Eeckhout, Persico & Todd, "A Theory of Optimal Random Crackdowns", *AER* 100(3), 1104-1135, 2010** (DOI 10.1257/aer.100.3.1104): "there are situations where it will be optimal for a crime-minimizing police agency to engage in random crackdowns"; crackdowns are *arbitrary and publicised* — they subject groups "not notably different from other groups in criminal propensities" to higher-intensity monitoring, and the groups are informed in advance; tested on Belgian speeding data; an earlier draft notes crackdowns would not arise if policing were decentralised. — [BSE](https://bse.eu/research/publications/theory-optimal-random-crackdowns)
- Lazear's speeding model (details in section 4) is the same object from the announced side: with G police on Z miles, unannounced patrol deters speeding everywhere iff K·G/Z ≥ V; otherwise the driver "speeds on all" miles, and announcing locations at least deters on patrolled miles. — [NBER WP 10932](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10932)

### Inferences
- Mixed strategies arise because inspection is a hide-and-seek game: a pure inspection schedule is a public best-response target. The leadership results (von Stengel; Tambe) show the evaluator gains from *credible commitment to a randomised schedule* rather than from secrecy per se: once the inspectee believes the randomisation, it complies in pure strategies while inspections remain.
- The sequential/recursive structure (von Stengel) is the closest formal model of "test the builder each round with a limited budget of checks": the inspector's optimal per-round inspection probability depends only on inspections and periods remaining, not on past successes, which is what makes round-by-round design tractable.
- Audit games add the lever an evaluator actually has — the size of the penalty on a failed check — and show it must be chosen jointly with the inspection probabilities.

### Gaps
- I could not access the full Handbook chapter or Dresher's original, so the closed-form mixed-strategy probabilities of the basic 2×2 inspection game and of Dresher's recursion are not quoted; the von Stengel abstract confirms the recursive structure and binomial-sum solution only.

## 3. Goodhart's law formally: Manheim-Garrabrant, Holmström-Milgrom multitasking, strategic classification, performative prediction, reward hacking, Campbell's law

### Takeaway
Goodhart failures split into four mechanisms (regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial; Manheim-Garrabrant 2018). Economics formalised the adversarial case in 1991 as multitask moral hazard (Holmström-Milgrom): rewarding the measurable task diverts effort from the unmeasurable one, so low-powered or fixed pay is optimal. CS formalised it as strategic classification (agents move features at cost; strategy-proof classifiers are NP-hard in general) and performative prediction (the deployed predictor changes the distribution it predicts). Skalse et al. (NeurIPS 2022) prove that for all stochastic policies a non-constant proxy is always hackable.

### Cited Findings
- **Manheim & Garrabrant, "Categorizing Variants of Goodhart's Law", arXiv 1803.04585 (cs.AI), v1 Mar 2018, v4 Feb 2019**: four categories. *Regressional*: selecting on an imperfect proxy also selects for its noise ("tails come apart"), M = G + N(μ,σ²). *Extremal*: selection pushes the system where earlier relationships fail — *model insufficiency* (M = G(s)+G′(s)) and *change in regime* (G = M+x if M≤a, M+y if M>a). *Causal*: the regulator's intervention breaks the proxy-goal link — intervening on a shared cause, an intermediary, or the metric itself; plus misread-structure cases (ignored shared cause: Metric~N(X,σ_m²), Goal~N(X,σ_g²)). *Adversarial*: another agent reacts — *adversarial misalignment / Campbell's law* (M_R = G_R + X; M_A = G_A·X: the agent optimises its own correlated metric to hijack selection), *normal cobra effect* (the incentive changes the agent's goal, G_AR = G_A0 + M_R, and the agent alters the causal structure), *non-causal cobra effect* (same incentive, but the harm comes via agent-side extremal/regressional pressure). Importance scales with "the amount of power directed towards optimizing the proxy". — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1803.04585); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.04585)
- **Holmström & Milgrom, "Multitask Principal-Agent Analyses: Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design", *JLEO* 7 (special issue), 24-52, 1991**: extends the principal-agent model to multiple tasks to explain "fixed wages in firms", asset ownership, job design and allocation of authority; claims to explain "fixed pay even when objective output measures exist" and bureaucratic constraints. — [Duke-hosted PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf); [edegan summary](https://edegan.com/wiki/Holmstrom_Milgrom_(1991)_-_Multitask_Principal_Agent_Analyses) (venue/aims confirmed; the formal theorem statements were not retrieved)
- Ederer-Holden-Meyer's taxonomy of gaming in a multitask model (published 2018): "1) diversion of effort away from activities which are socially valuable but difficult to measure and reward, towards activities that are easily measured and rewarded; 2) exploitation of the rules of classification to improve apparent, though not actual, performance; and 3) distortion of choices about timing". — [Nuffield PDF](https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/Users/Meyer/ehmapr02.pdf)
- **Hardt, Megiddo, Papadimitriou & Wootters, "Strategic Classification", ITCS 2016, pp. 111-122 (arXiv 1506.06980, cs.LG)**: "the Jury designs a classifier, and the Contestant receives an input to the classifier, which he may change at some cost"; "For a natural class of cost functions, we obtain computationally efficient learning algorithms which are near-optimal. For general cost functions, designing an approximately optimal strategy-proof classifier, for inverse-polynomial approximation, is NP-hard." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06980)
- **Perdomo, Zrnic, Mendler-Dünner & Hardt, "Performative Prediction", ICML 2020, PMLR 119:7599-7609 (arXiv 2002.06673)**: predictions are *performative* when "the prediction influences the target"; *performative stability* means predictions "are calibrated not against past outcomes, but against the future outcomes that manifest from acting on the prediction"; the framework "strictly subsumes the setting known as strategic classification" and gives "the first sufficient conditions for retraining to overcome strategic feedback effects". — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/perdomo20a.html)
- **Kleinberg & Raghavan, "How Do Classifiers Induce Agents to Invest Effort Strategically?", EC 2019 (arXiv 1807.05307; AAAI 2020 sister track as "Designing Evaluation Rules That Are Robust to Strategic Behavior")**: agents invest effort across features; a "tight characterization" of when a classifier can steer agents toward real improvement rather than gaming; "a simple linear mechanism suffices" whenever any "reasonable" mechanism works. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.05307); [AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/7118)
- **Skalse, Howe, Krasheninnikov & Krueger, "Defining and Characterizing Reward Hacking" (NeurIPS 2022 listing title: "Reward Gaming"), arXiv 2209.13085 (v1 Sept 2022, rev. Mar 2025)**: reward hacking = optimising an imperfect proxy lowers true return; a proxy is *unhackable* if raising its expected return can never lower the true expected return; "For all stochastic policies, two reward functions can only be unhackable if one of them is constant"; non-trivial unhackable pairs exist only for deterministic policies or finite policy sets; conclusion: narrow task specification and value alignment pull in opposite directions. — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/3d719fee332caa23d5038b8a90e81796-Abstract-Conference.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.13085)
- Campbell, "Assessing the impact of planned social change", *Evaluation and Program Planning* 2(1), 67-90, 1979 (a 1976 version also circulates): "the more a quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures"; example of crime statistics corrupted by under-recording and downgrading. — [Wikipedia, secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell%27s_law)

### Inferences
- The Manheim-Garrabrant adversarial branch is exactly Holmström-Milgrom's object; the mapping is: rubric = M_R, builder's own objective = G_A, and the "cobra" variants correspond to the builder changing the production technology to hit the rubric. The regressional and extremal branches survive even with a non-strategic builder — they are selection effects of iterating on a proxy — so an evaluator refining a rubric round by round faces Goodhart even if the builder is honest.
- Skalse et al.'s theorem is the sharpest statement that no fixed proxy rubric is safe against a builder that randomises or searches broadly; it motivates *changing* the proxy (the moving-criteria question in section 5) as the only route left, which is also what performative prediction's retraining results study.
- Hardt et al.'s NP-hardness means "design a gaming-proof rubric once" is not generally feasible; Kleinberg-Raghavan's result says that when *some* mechanism induces real improvement, a linear one does — a useful restriction on rubric shape.

### Gaps
- Holmström-Milgrom's formal results (the optimal-incentive formula with multiple tasks and the condition under which incentives on measurable tasks should be zero) were not retrieved verbatim; only the paper's venue and aims are sourced here.
- Campbell's original text is sourced only through a secondary summary.

## 4. Hidden versus revealed evaluation criteria: should the grading rule, test, or audit criteria be disclosed?

### Takeaway
The economics answer is conditional, not "always hide". Lazear: announce the test when learning or monitoring is costly (you then at least get the announced items), keep it vague when learners are able or enforcement is plentiful. Ederer-Holden-Meyer: opacity about incentive weights balances effort across tasks but loads risk on the agent; it wins when the agent's private task preference is weak, risk aversion is significant, tasks are complementary, or measurement errors are correlated/small. Frankel-Kartik and Ball: when agents manipulate data, the optimal rule deliberately *under-uses* the data and down-weights manipulable features, and a committed scoring intermediary beats full disclosure. Perez-Richet-Skreta: against covert falsification the optimal test sets a stricter operational standard and gives non-compliant types a positive pass probability to deter falsification. Ghalme et al. show a positive "price of opacity" in strategic classification and recommend transparency under a sufficient condition.

### Cited Findings
- **Lazear, "Speeding, Terrorism, and Teaching to the Test", *QJE* 121(3), 1029-1061, 2006** (NBER WP 10932, Nov 2004, as "Speeding, Tax Fraud, and Teaching to the Test"): "the optimal rules for high-stakes testing depend on the costs of learning and of monitoring. For high cost learners, and when monitoring technology is inefficient, it is better to announce what will be tested. For efficient learners, de-emphasizing the test itself is the right strategy." Speeding model: Z miles, G police, fine K, speeding gain V; unannounced patrol deters iff K·G/Z ≥ V, else "if the driver chooses to speed on one mile, he speeds on all"; "When police are abundant or when the fine is high relative to the benefit from speeding, it is better to keep police locations secret, which results in obeying the law everywhere"; implication: "tests should be clearly defined for younger children, but more amorphous for more advanced students". — [NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10932.pdf); [QJE record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v121y2006i3p1029-1061..html)
- **Ederer, Holden & Meyer, "Gaming and Strategic Opacity in Incentive Provision", *RAND Journal of Economics* 49(4), 819-854, 2018** (Cowles DP 1935, CEPR DP 9319): two-task moral hazard; the agent privately knows which task is cheaper; "opaque" schemes "make the agent uncertain ex ante about the incentive coefficients in the linear payment rule"; "Relative to transparent menus of linear contracts, these opaque schemes induce more balanced efforts, but they also impose more risk on the agent per unit of aggregate effort induced"; in specific settings optimal opaque schemes "not only strictly dominate the best transparent menu but also eliminate the efficiency losses from the agent's hidden information"; opacity is more likely preferred when "(i) the agent's privately known preference between the tasks is weak; (ii) the agent's risk aversion is significant; (iii) efforts on the tasks are highly complementary for the principal; or (iv) the errors in measuring performance have large correlation or small variance". Their proof of Proposition 4 shows an opaque "EAR" (equal-probability random weights) scheme is strictly beaten by a suitably designed transparent balanced contract in one region, i.e. the result is genuinely two-sided. The paper traces the idea to Bentham (1830) on "impossibilizing the knowledge as to what part the field of exercise the trial will be applied to" and to Bevan & Hood (BMJ 2004) proposing "more randomness in the assessment of performance, at the expense of transparency", analogised to "unseen examinations". — [Nuffield PDF](https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/Users/Meyer/ehmapr02.pdf); [RAND record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/randje/v49y2018i4p819-854.html)
- **Frankel & Kartik, "Muddled Information", *JPE* 127(4), 1739-1776, 2019** (DOI 10.1086/701604): agents differ in a *natural action* and in *gaming ability*; "As incentives to take higher actions increase—due to higher stakes or more easily manipulated signaling technology—more information is revealed about gaming ability, and less about natural actions"; new externality: showing actions to extra observers can worsen information for existing observers; applications: credit scoring, school testing, web search. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/doi10.1086-701604.html)
- **Frankel & Kartik, "Improving Information from Manipulable Data", *JEEA* 20, 79-115, 2022** (arXiv 1908.10330): "making allocations more responsive to data ... amplifies manipulation and makes the data less informative"; the remedy is for the decision-maker to commit to use the data *less* than it could ("flatten" the allocation rule), which improves accuracy. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10330); [Simons talk abstract](https://simons.berkeley.edu/talks/improving-muddled-information)
- **Ball, "Scoring Strategic Agents", *AEJ: Microeconomics* 17(1), 97-129, 2025** (arXiv 1909.01888, v1 Sept 2019, v6 May 2024; earlier title "Incentive-Compatible Prediction"): an intermediary builds a score from several features the sender can manipulate at privately known cost; the accuracy-maximising scoring rule "gives less weight to some features to discourage manipulation, and more weight to others so the score is accurate on average"; "The receiver prefers this rule to full disclosure because it eases his commitment problem." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01888); [AEJ record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejmic/v17y2025i1p97-129.html)
- **Perez-Richet & Skreta, "Test Design under Falsification", *Econometrica* 90(3), 1109-1142, May 2022** (DOI 10.3982/ECTA16346): a receiver approves/rejects on a test signal and wants to approve only states above a baseline standard; the agent can *covertly* falsify the state at a cost; receiver-optimal tests "set a stricter operational standard and give noncompliant states a positive chance of approval, which deters them from falsifying up to the standard"; falsification-detection technology helps by letting the designer "build an implicit cost of falsification into the test through lower signal values", which needs a richer signal space. A seminar abstract adds that under covert falsification the receiver-optimal test is inefficient and a falsification-proof test makes the agent strictly better off. — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2022/05/01/test-design-under-falsification); [UC Chile seminar abstract](https://economia.uc.cl/events/eduardo-perez-richet-sciences-po/)
- **Ghalme, Nair, Eilat, Talgam-Cohen & Rosenfeld, "Strategic Classification in the Dark", ICML 2021, PMLR 139:3672-3681 (arXiv 2102.11592)**: strategic classification when the classifier is hidden; defines the *price of opacity* (change in prediction error when the policy is opaque rather than transparent); under a sufficient condition this price is strictly positive, and in that case the authors recommend transparency; experiments on how Hardt et al.'s robust classifier behaves with agents in the dark. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/ghalme21a.html)
- Lizzeri, "Information Revelation and Certification Intermediaries", *RAND* 30(2), 214-231, 1999: a monopoly certifier's optimum is "to reveal only whether quality is above some minimal standard", yet it "can capture a large share of the surplus"; competing certifiers push toward full disclosure. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/randje/v30y1999isummerp214-231.html)
- Eeckhout-Persico-Todd (see section 2): optimal crackdowns are *publicised in advance*, i.e. announced randomisation can be optimal. — [BSE](https://bse.eu/research/publications/theory-optimal-random-crackdowns)

### Inferences
- The literature separates three things an evaluator could hide: (i) *which items* are checked (Lazear; crackdowns) — hide when monitoring capacity is high relative to the gain from shirking, announce when it is low; (ii) *the weights* on criteria (EHM) — hide when the builder's informational advantage about which criterion is cheap to satisfy is small and the evaluator cares about balance; (iii) *the mapping from features to score* (Frankel-Kartik; Ball) — the optimum is not secrecy but a committed, deliberately flattened rule that under-weights manipulable features, and commitment to it is what dominates full disclosure.
- Perez-Richet-Skreta is the closest model to "the builder can fake passing": the counter-intuitive instruction is to raise the bar *and* occasionally pass non-compliant work, because a deterministic fail at the standard makes faking up to the standard worthwhile.
- Ghalme et al. is the ML-side caution that hiding the classifier can cost accuracy when agents respond to a mis-estimated rule — opacity is not free even on the evaluator's own objective.

### Gaps
- No paper found that treats the *partial* revelation of a rubric (reveal some criteria, hide others) as a design variable in its own right; the closest are EHM (random weights) and Lazear (announce a subset of items). I did not find a "transparency of standards" literature under that name in economics.

## 5. Evaluation criteria that change over time: the uninformed principal, preference drift, criteria constructed by observing outputs

### Takeaway
This literature is thin. I found no economic-theory paper in which a principal *discovers her own objective* by observing the agent's outputs and then contracts on it. The nearest formal neighbours are: robust contracting when the principal does not know the agent's technology (Carroll), dynamic persuasion where the principal's private information is persistent and disclosed over time (Zhao et al.), order-of-play reversal when the evaluator updates faster than the agents (Zrnic et al.), and performative prediction's retraining dynamics. The behavioural foundation that preferences are "constructed, not merely revealed" is Slovic (1995); the software-engineering phrase IKIWISI is attested but I could not confirm Boehm as its source.

### Cited Findings
- Slovic, "The Construction of Preference", *American Psychologist* 50, 364-371, 1995: preferences "are often constructed—not merely revealed—in the process of elicitation"; evidence is that "normatively equivalent methods of elicitation often give rise to systematically different responses", violating procedure invariance; expanded in Lichtenstein & Slovic (eds.), *The Construction of Preference*, Cambridge UP 2006. — [U. Oregon repository](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/items/8bfbe1ef-a008-470a-a730-625bfc00c192)
- IKIWISI ("I'll know it when I see it") appears in software-requirements texts as the reason users "may reject the first attempt at a system"; the search did not link the term to Boehm. — [O'Reilly, *Software Requirements* 3rd ed., ch. 15](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/software-requirements-3rd/9780735679658/ch15.html)
- Carroll, "Robustness and Linear Contracts", *AER* 2015: the principal "is uncertain as to what the agent can and cannot do, and evaluates possible contracts by their worst-case performance"; the optimal contract is linear. — [author PDF](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/gabriel.carroll/robustcontract-published.pdf)
- Zhao, Mezzetti, Renou & Tomala (arXiv 2007.05983): principal's private information is persistent and disclosed gradually as the sole incentive tool; stopping rule as in section 1. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.05983v3)
- **Zrnic, Mazumdar, Sastry & Jordan, "Who Leads and Who Follows in Strategic Classification?", NeurIPS 2021 (arXiv 2106.12529)**: standard strategic classification is Stackelberg with the decision-maker leading; "a decision-maker that makes updates faster than the agents can reverse the order of play, meaning that the agents lead and the decision-maker follows"; this reversal "can be desirable for both"; "a decision-maker with the freedom to choose their update frequency can induce learning dynamics that converge to Stackelberg equilibria with either order of play". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.12529)
- Perdomo et al. (section 3): retraining dynamics and sufficient conditions for convergence to a performatively stable point when the distribution responds to the deployed rule. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/perdomo20a.html)
- Hörner & Lambert (section 1): effort-maximising ratings aggregate "information of different types and vintages", i.e. the criterion applied at date t is a designed function of the history, and effort can lower some future ratings. — [TSE](https://www.tse-fr.eu/articles/motivational-ratings)
- (recent, 2026) Chen & Liu (section 1): optimal dynamic disclosure is two one-shot releases, optimistic then pessimistic. — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2601.13686)

### Inferences
- The closest *formal* analogue of "the rubric is refined each round after seeing outputs" is Zrnic et al.'s update-frequency result: if the evaluator revises faster than the builder adapts, the builder effectively becomes the leader and the evaluator the follower, which the authors show can be preferable. Combined with Perdomo et al., the natural concept is a performatively stable rubric rather than a fixed one.
- Slovic's constructed-preference result gives the behavioural reason the principal's criterion is path-dependent (the elicitation order — here, which outputs were seen first — changes the stated criterion), but no theory paper I found carries that into a contracting model.

### Gaps
- No source found for a principal whose objective is learned from the agent's outputs ("uninformed principal", "preference drift", "evolving objective" searches returned robust-contracting and screening papers instead). This should be reported as an open area rather than a mature literature.
- Boehm attribution of IKIWISI unverified; Lichtenstein-Slovic 2006 volume seen only as a catalogue entry.

## 6. Repeated-game aspects: subjective evaluation, relational contracts, coarse feedback and money burning

### Takeaway
When the evaluator's assessment is private and unverifiable, the agent must be given reason to believe honest reports; the classic results show the optimal contract compresses pay, uses bonuses rather than dismissal (MacLeod 2003), withholds feedback until termination with "no loss of generality" (Fuchs 2007), and requires destroying surplus (money burning or inefficient termination) after the worst histories, with the amount burned independent of horizon and discounting. Subjective and objective measures interact: a near-perfect objective measure can destroy the relational contract by raising the evaluator's temptation to renege (Baker-Gibbons-Murphy 1994). Ratings designers can sustain reputation only through censoring when a commitment type is possible (Ekmekci 2011).

### Cited Findings
- **Baker, Gibbons & Murphy, "Subjective Performance Measures in Optimal Incentive Contracts", *QJE* 109(4), 1125-1156, 1994** (NBER WP 4480, 1993): subjective measures sit in implicit (relational) contracts, objective in explicit ones; "when objective measures are nearly perfect, implicit contracts become infeasible because the firm gains more from reneging"; but better objective measures can also raise the value of the relationship and lower the temptation to renege; includes subjective weights on objective measures and case evidence. — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4480); [QJE record](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/oup/qjecon/v109y1994i4p1125-1156..html)
- **MacLeod, "Optimal Contracting with Subjective Evaluation", *AER* 93(1), 216-240, March 2003**: "The optimal contract results in more compressed pay relative to the case with verifiable performance measures"; "the optimal contract entails the use of bonus pay rather than the threat of dismissal, hence neither 'efficiency wages' nor the right to dismiss an employee are necessary ingredients"; discrimination lowers both pay and performance so measured discrimination understates true discrimination. — [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/optimal-contracting-with-subjective-evaluation/)
- MacLeod & Tan, "Optimal Contracting with Subjective Evaluation: The Effects of Timing, Malfeasance and Guile", NBER WP 22156, 2016, extends the 2003 model to timing and malfeasance. — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22156)
- **Fuchs, "Contracting with Repeated Moral Hazard and Private Evaluations", *AER* 97(4), 1432-1448, Sept 2007**: the principal privately observes output; "Restricting attention to contracts with a constant efficiency wage and no feedback until dismissal costs nothing in generality"; finite horizon: "The optimal contract requires burning resources, but only after the worst possible output sequence, and the amount burned does not depend on the horizon length or the discount factor"; infinite horizon: "a family of fixed-interval review contracts approaches first best as the discount factor goes to 1". — [IDEAS/RePEc (2005 WP abstract)](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wpa/wuwpga/0511007.html)
- **Ekmekci, "Sustainable Reputations with Rating Systems", *JET* 146(2), 479-503, 2011**: a long-lived seller faces short-lived buyers who see only a central rating, not past signals; "absent reputation effects, information censoring cannot improve attainable payoffs"; but if buyers put some probability on a commitment type, "there exists a rating system and an equilibrium ... such that the expected present discounted payoff of the seller is almost his Stackelberg payoff after every history", in contrast to Cripps-Mailath-Samuelson (2004) where reputations fade under full observation. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jetheo/v146y2011i2p479-503.html)
- Lizzeri (1999, section 4): the certifier's optimal disclosure is a pass/fail threshold. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/randje/v30y1999isummerp214-231.html)
- Boleslavsky-Kim's static result that fine feedback can lower effort (Corollary 5.2) and Hörner-Lambert's dynamic result that effort can hurt some future ratings are the information-design counterparts of coarse feedback. — [B-K PDF](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f115756/f115756.pdf); [TSE](https://www.tse-fr.eu/articles/motivational-ratings)

### Inferences
- Why coarse feedback and money burning: with private evaluation, every report that lowers the agent's pay must cost the evaluator too, or the evaluator would always report "bad"; the cheapest truthful punishment is therefore one that destroys surplus (termination, a third-party payment) and is triggered only after the worst histories (Fuchs). Reviewing at fixed intervals rather than continuously, and giving no interim feedback, is not a simplification but part of the optimum.
- For an evaluator whose verdicts are its own judgment, BGM's result is the warning that adding a very good objective metric can make the subjective part of the evaluation non-credible; the subjective component needs the relationship's continuation value to back it.
- Ekmekci's result ties evaluator reputation to censoring: a rating mechanism that reveals less than it knows can be what keeps the evaluated party's incentives alive over an indefinite horizon.

### Gaps
- Levin's "Relational Incentive Contracts" (*AER* 2003), which gives the stationary-contract and termination results for subjective evaluation, was not searched for directly in this run and is not cited here; treat it as a known missing entry in this section.
- MacLeod 2003's explicit money-burning (payment to a third party) construction is described in the abstract only as "bonus pay"; the third-party-payment detail is from recollection and is not sourced above, so it is omitted from the findings.
