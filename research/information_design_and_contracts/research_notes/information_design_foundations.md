## Key question 1: The founding papers and their roots — what exactly do they prove?

### Takeaway
Kamenica & Gentzkow (2011, AER) is the founding paper: it defines the single-sender, single-receiver commitment model, proves that Bayes plausibility is the only constraint on inducible posterior distributions, and shows the sender's value is the concave closure (concavification) of his indirect utility at the prior. The mathematics comes from Aumann & Maschler's 1966-68 arms-control reports (published as the 1995 MIT Press book); Brocas & Carrillo (2007, RAND) and Rayo & Segal (2010, JPE) are the nearest contemporaneous precursors with restricted signal spaces or specific preferences.

### Cited Findings

**Kamenica & Gentzkow (2011), "Bayesian Persuasion," American Economic Review 101(6): 2590-2615.**
- Abstract: a symmetric-information model where a sender chooses a signal to reveal to a receiver who then takes a noncontractible action; the paper derives necessary and sufficient conditions for a signal that strictly benefits the sender, characterises sender-optimal signals, studies comparative statics in preference alignment, and applies the results to litigators, lobbyists and salespeople (JEL D72, D82, D83, K40, M31) — [KG 2011](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Prosecutor-judge example: prior Pr(guilty)=0.3; judge gets 1 for the just action, prosecutor gets 1 for conviction regardless of state. No information: always acquit. Full information: convict 30% of the time. Uniquely optimal signal: π(i|innocent)=4/7, π(g|innocent)=3/7, π(g|guilty)=1, which yields conviction with probability 60% even though 70% of defendants are innocent and the judge knows the investigation was designed to maximise conviction — [KG 2011, pp. 2590-2591](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Model primitives: receiver utility u(a,ω), sender utility v(a,ω), common prior μ0 in the interior of Δ(Ω), A compact, Ω finite (extended to compact metric spaces in the online appendix). A signal is a finite realization space plus a family of distributions {π(·|ω)}. Solution concept: sender-preferred subgame perfect equilibrium (receiver breaks indifference in sender's favour) — [KG 2011, Sec. I.A](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Key assumption: the sender cannot distort or conceal the realization once it is known; this abstracts from the incentive-compatibility issues of earlier strategic-communication models — [KG 2011, p. 2591](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Proposition 1 (equivalence): (i) a signal with value v* exists; (ii) a *straightforward* signal (realizations are action recommendations the receiver follows) with value v* exists; (iii) a Bayes-plausible distribution of posteriors τ with E_τ v̂(μ)=v* exists. The (i)-(ii) equivalence is "closely analogous to the revelation principle (Myerson 1979)"; the (i)-(iii) direction is constructive: π(s|ω)=μ_s(ω)τ(μ_s)/μ0(ω) — [KG 2011, Prop. 1](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Corollary 1: sender benefits from persuasion iff there is a Bayes-plausible τ with E_τ v̂(μ) > v̂(μ0); the value of an optimal signal is max_τ E_τ v̂(μ) s.t. E_τ μ = μ0 — [KG 2011, Cor. 1](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Corollary 2: the value of an optimal signal is V(μ0), where V(μ)=sup{z | (μ,z) ∈ co(v̂)} is the concave closure, "the smallest concave function that is everywhere weakly greater than v̂"; the sender benefits iff V(μ0) > v̂(μ0). Footnote 7: Aumann & Maschler (1995) call V the concavification of v̂ — [KG 2011, Cor. 2 and fn. 7](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- In the example, v̂ is a step function (0 below μ=0.5, 1 at or above), the optimal τ puts 0.4 on μ=0 and 0.6 on μ=0.5, so 0.3 = 0.4×0 + 0.6×0.5 and the value is 0.6; V(μ)=2μ for μ≤0.5 and 1 above — [KG 2011, Fig. 2](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Remark 1: if v̂ is concave the sender never benefits for any prior; if v̂ is convex and not concave he benefits for every prior — [KG 2011, Remark 1](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Proposition 2: if there is no "information Sender would share" (no μ with v̂(μ) > E_μ v(â(μ0),ω)), the sender does not benefit; if there is such information and the receiver's preference is *discrete* at the prior (preferred action bounded away from the others), the sender benefits; with finite A, discreteness at the prior holds generically — [KG 2011, Prop. 2](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Proposition 3: when the sender's payoff depends only on the expected state, the sender benefits iff Ṽ(E_μ0 ω) > ṽ(E_μ0 ω), where ṽ is the payoff as a function of the posterior mean; but Ṽ(E_μ0 ω) is only an upper bound on the value, because not every distribution of posterior means averaging to the prior mean is Bayes plausible (counter-example with A=[0,1], Ω={-1,0,1}, v=a²) — [KG 2011, Prop. 3 and Sec. III](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Full vs no disclosure: "if v̂ is (strictly) concave, no disclosure is (uniquely) optimal, and if v̂ is (strictly) convex, full disclosure is (uniquely) optimal" — [KG 2011, Sec. III](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Proposition 4: if an optimal signal induces a belief leading to the sender's worst action, the receiver is certain of her action at that belief. Proposition 5 (under Assumption 1): at any interior induced belief, or any belief leading to a best-attainable action, the receiver's preference is not discrete (with finite A: she is indifferent between two actions) — [KG 2011, Props. 4-5](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Preference alignment (Sec. IV): more aligned preferences weakly raise the sender's utility, but the gain from persuasion and the informativeness of the optimal signal can go either way; "in contrast to previous work on strategic communication, ... making preferences more aligned can reduce the extent of communication" — [KG 2011, Sec. IV](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Lobbying example: politician chooses a∈[0,1], state ω∈[0,1], lobbyist's bliss point αω+(1-α)ω* with ω*>1; v̂ is convex iff α>1/2 so full disclosure is uniquely optimal for α>1/2, no disclosure for α<1/2, all signals equal at α=1/2, and the optimum is independent of ω* — [KG 2011, Sec. V.A](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Commitment: the full-commitment game has the same equilibrium outcomes as a game where the sender privately observes s and sends a verifiable message m ∋ s (proved in the online appendix); and the sender's gain under commitment weakly bounds his gain in any alternative communication game, so the results give "an upper bound on gains from communication ... regardless of Sender's commitment power" — [KG 2011, Sec. I.C](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Extensions (Sec. VI): receiver private information about the state or about preferences is handled by integrating it into v̂ (eq. 3), so Props 1 and Cors 1-2 still hold; multiple receivers are easy either with separable utility and own-action concerns or with public signals only, but the third class (receivers care about each other's actions and private signals) breaks the reduction to posterior distributions — [KG 2011, Sec. VI](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)

**Roots named in KG 2011 and Kamenica 2019.**
- KG 2011 names Aumann & Maschler (1995) and Brocas & Carrillo (2007) as the closest related work: Aumann-Maschler "employ formal methods very similar to ours" for repeated zero-sum games with one-sided incomplete information, where the informed player's actions serve as a signal and Nash equilibrium implicitly allows commitment; Brocas-Carrillo study a binary state with a sequence of symmetric binary signals — [KG 2011, p. 2593](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Kamenica 2019: the concavification approach was developed in Aumann & Maschler (1966), "Game theoretic aspects of gradual disarmament," Report ST-80 Ch. 5 to the US Arms Control and Disarmament Agency, written 1966-68 by Aumann, Maschler and Stearns, collected as *Repeated Games with Incomplete Information* (MIT Press 1995); for half a century it stayed inside repeated-game theory, and "formulating a model with an explicit information design step seems to have been important for stimulating further research" — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 3.1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- The Aumann-Maschler "Cav u" theorem: the value of the n-stage repeated game with one-sided incomplete information satisfies Cav u(p) ≤ V_n(p) ≤ Cav u(p) + c/√n, and the infinite-game value is Cav u(p), where Cav u is the least concave majorant of the non-revealing game value — [Tomala lecture slides](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~sri/games2020/Slides/tomala.pdf); a recent paper states that both Bayes plausibility and the concavification characterisation "were adapted from the work of Aumann, Maschler and Stearns (1995)" — [arXiv 2209.06536](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.06536)
- Brocas & Carrillo (2007), "Influence through Ignorance," *RAND Journal of Economics* 38(4): 931-947: the sender chooses how many IID draws of a fixed binary signal to generate; Kamenica notes that concavification applies only with the unrestricted signal space, which is why the KG model became standard — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 3.1 and refs](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf); the paper's idea is that a party can control public opinion by stopping the flow of public information (e.g. a drug firm that skips follow-up trials), at the cost of staying ignorant itself — [phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news125585969.html)
- Rayo & Segal (2010), "Optimal Information Disclosure," *Journal of Political Economy* 118(5): 949-987: contemporaneous with KG; a sender commits to a disclosure rule about prospects, the receiver (with private information about her preferences / outside option) accepts or rejects; profit is typically highest under partial disclosure that pools less relevant but more profitable prospects with more relevant ones, while receiver welfare is highest under full disclosure — [Rayo-Segal NBER 2009 version](https://m.nber.org/conferences/2009/MDs09/rayo.pdf); [Kamenica 2019 fn. 15](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Other precursors named in KG 2011: Ostrovsky & Schwarz (2010, AEJ Micro 2(2):34-63) on grade transcripts under school competition; Hörner & Skrzypacz (2005) on sequential partial revelation to sell information; Shmaya & Yariv (2009) on which sequences of posterior distributions are Bayes-consistent; Ganuza & Penalva (2010) on orders over signals via posterior dispersion — [KG 2011, pp. 2593, 2596](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "founding" status of KG 2011 rests on three things that the precursors lacked together: an unrestricted signal space, an explicit design step with commitment, and the general concavification characterisation; Aumann-Maschler had the mathematics, Brocas-Carrillo the design step with a restricted space, Rayo-Segal a specific preference structure.
- Proposition 1's (i)-(ii) equivalence is the single-receiver seed of the obedience/recommendation principle that Bergemann-Morris later make the organising device for many receivers.

- I could not access the KG 2011 online appendix (the compact-metric-space extension and the verifiable-message equivalence proof) directly; the claims above are from the main text's description of it.
- The Brocas-Carrillo RAND article itself was not retrievable; its description relies on KG 2011, Kamenica 2019 and a news summary.

## Key question 2: The central results — Bayes plausibility, concavification, obedience/LP formulation, full vs no disclosure

### Takeaway
Two reformulations dominate the field: the belief-based one (choose a Bayes-plausible distribution of posteriors; value = concavification at the prior; KG 2011) and the outcome-based one (choose a joint distribution over states and action recommendations subject to linear obedience constraints; Bergemann-Morris 2016). No disclosure is optimal when the sender's indirect utility is concave, full disclosure when it is convex; in the "posterior-mean" case concavification fails and linear-programming / duality methods take over.

### Cited Findings
- Bayes plausibility, formal statement: τ is Bayes plausible if E_{μ~τ} μ = μ0; every signal induces a Bayes-plausible τ by iterated expectations, and "Bayes plausibility is the only restriction": for every Bayes-plausible τ there is a π with τ=⟨π⟩, constructively π(s|ω)=μ(ω)τ(μ)/μ0(ω) — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 3.1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- The reformulated problem is max_τ E_{μ~τ} v̂(μ) s.t. E_τ μ = μ0 (eq. 2); the concavification of v̂ at μ0 equals max{z | (μ0,z) ∈ co(v̂)}, so "Sender's payoff under the optimal signal is precisely the concavification of v̂ evaluated at the prior"; with two or three states one reads the optimal posteriors off the graph, with more states the approach gives only qualitative features — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 3.1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Binary signals suffice when the state is binary: the optimal binary signal is the one whose support is {μ_l*, μ_h*} on the concavification, and "a signal with more than two realizations cannot improve Sender's payoff" (binary-state case, Fig. 1) — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 3.1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Signal-size bound: it suffices that |S| ≥ min{|A|, |Ω|}; with private signals to n receivers and designer-favourable selection, |S| ≥ |A1×...×An| suffices by a revelation-principle argument, but action recommendations no longer suffice under adversarial equilibrium selection (Mathevet et al.) — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 5](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Obedience / LP formulation (single receiver): describe behaviour by a decision rule giving the probability of each action in each state; the rule is *obedient* if the receiver always wants to follow the (stochastic) recommendation; in the bank-run example (states G,B equally likely, stay pays y in G and -1 in B, 0<y<1), obedience requires γ_B ≥ 1−y + yγ_G, so staying for sure is never attainable and the regulator's best outcome is γ_G=0, γ_B=1−y, implemented by a "bad" signal with probability 1−y in the bad state and a "good" signal otherwise; "any obedient decision rule can always arise when the depositor is given a signal equal to his action recommendation." The authors explicitly note they "do not appeal to a concavification argument" — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 2.1 and fn. 2](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- Revelation-principle duality: "For the mechanism design problem, we can restrict attention to direct mechanisms where the players' action sets are equal their possible types. Conversely, for the information design problem, we can restrict attention to information structures where the players' type sets are equal to their action sets." — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 1](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- The two-step procedure: first characterise the set of outcomes attainable under some information structure (the obedient / BCE set), then let the designer pick the best one — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Secs. 1 and 3](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf); Kamenica: the BCE characterisation "involves deriving a set of linear constraints, each of which requires that a receiver prefers an action recommended by her signal realization over all other actions" — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 46](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Full vs no disclosure rule: concave v̂ → no disclosure; convex v̂ → full disclosure (strict versions unique) — [KG 2011, Sec. III](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf); when v does not depend on the state, v̂(μ)=v(â(μ)) and the question reduces to whether â(μ) and v(a) are concave or convex — [KG 2011, after Remark 1](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Posterior-mean special case (Ω=[0,1], action depends on E_μ ω): concavification of ṽ does not solve the problem because not every distribution of posterior means averaging to the prior mean is inducible; if the concavification of ṽ is strictly above ṽ at the prior mean, no information is strictly suboptimal (KG 2011 Prop. 3) — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 3.2 and fn. 17](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Gentzkow & Kamenica (2016), "A Rothschild-Stiglitz approach to Bayesian persuasion," *AER P&P* 106(5): 597-601: represent each signal by c_π(x)=∫_0^x G_π(t)dt where G_π is the CDF of posterior means; π is Blackwell more informative than π′ iff c_π ≥ c_π′; every convex function sandwiched between the no-information and full-information curves is induced by some signal, so the set of signals becomes a set of convex functions and finite-action problems are solved by minimising a left derivative — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 3.2](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Kolotilin (2018), "Optimal information disclosure: a linear programming approach," *Theoretical Economics* 13:607-635, and Dworczak & Martini (2019), "The simple economics of optimal persuasion," *JPE* 127(5):1993-2048, "draw on linear programming methods and represent Sender's problem as a consumer-choice situation where Sender purchases posterior means using the prior as her endowment"; Kolotilin assumes binary actions with receiver private preference information, Dworczak-Martini allow general actions and "derive a simple way to verify whether a given signal is optimal"; Ivanov (2015) and Dworczak-Martini give necessary and sufficient conditions for monotone partitional signals; Mensch (2018) for monotone partitions without the posterior-mean assumption — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 3.2 and fn. 23](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Dworczak-Martini abstract: an analogy between the sender's problem and Walrasian equilibria of a "persuasion economy" in which the sender buys posteriors at prices and a single firm garbles the state; welfare theorems verify optimality and characterise supporting prices — [SUFE newsletter abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/346065); the later Dworczak-Kolotilin (TE 2024) survey of duality describes the optimal dual variable as "a price function on the state space ... a supergradient of the concave closure of the objective at the prior" — [Dworczak & Kolotilin 2024, TE](https://econtheory.org/ojs/index.php/te/article/viewFile/20241701/40656/1253)
- Computation: Dughmi & Xu (2016, STOC) show a polynomial-time algorithm under IID symmetry across actions, a poly(|A|,1/ε) algorithm for ε-optimal signals with ε-rational receivers, and that computing the sender's maximal payoff in general is #P-hard; Dughmi (2017, SIGecom Exchanges 15(2)) surveys the algorithmic literature — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 3.3](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Graduate courses teach exactly these objects: Condorelli's PhD syllabus covers "the information design problem, the Splitting Lemma, and Kamenica and Gentzkow's concavification solution ... Blackwell's theorem, costly information, and extreme-point results" with Kleiner, Moldovanu & Strack (2021, *Econometrica*) "Extreme Points and Majorization" as a core reference — [Condorelli syllabus 2024](https://economia.uniroma2.it/public/phdef/files/Condorelli_Syllabus_2024.pdf); Krähmer's Berlin School of Economics short course runs "information structures and posterior beliefs, concavification, and BP as a linear problem" then "Bayes correlated equilibrium" — [BSoE course page](https://berlinschoolofeconomics.de/phd-program/courses/detail/bsoe-short-course-information-design)

### Inferences
- The belief-based and outcome-based formulations are the same LP viewed in different coordinates: KG's Proposition 1(ii) already says straightforward (obedient) signals are without loss; Bergemann-Morris make the obedience constraints the primary object because they survive the move to many receivers, where posterior distributions do not.
- The "posterior-mean" literature (Rothschild-Stiglitz curves, Kolotilin LP, Dworczak-Martini prices, later majorization) is best read as the field's standard toolkit for continuous states, replacing concavification where the latter silently fails.

## Key question 3: From one receiver to many — Bayes correlated equilibrium, public vs private signals, Taneva, Mathevet-Perego-Taneva

### Takeaway
Bergemann & Morris define Bayes correlated equilibrium (BCE) as the set of joint distributions over states, initial signals and action profiles satisfying obedience; it equals the set of outcomes attainable by any information structure, so information design with many receivers is "select the best BCE". Taneva (2019) and Mathevet-Perego-Taneva (2020) supply the designer's optimisation layer, the latter via a concave-envelope representation over belief hierarchies that also handles adversarial equilibrium selection.

### Cited Findings
- Bergemann & Morris (2013), "Robust predictions in games with incomplete information," *Econometrica* 81(4): 1251-1308; Bergemann & Morris (2016a), "Bayes correlated equilibrium and the comparison of information structures in games," *Theoretical Economics* 11: 487-522 — [Kamenica 2019 refs](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf); the TE paper "characterizes the set of outcomes that can arise in Bayes Nash equilibrium when players observe the given information structure plus possibly additional signals, which it calls Bayes correlated equilibria" — [Princeton publication record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/bayes-correlated-equilibrium-and-the-comparison-of-information-st/)
- BCE definition in words: "a joint distribution of states, initial information signals and action profiles that satisfy the relevant obedience constraints: that is, the constraint that each player is always choosing a best response given that he knows his initial signal and the action that he is going to play"; "this set clearly reduces to the set of outcomes attainable by omniscient persuasion in the one player case" — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 3](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- Unifying map: many players + designer with no informational advantage = communication in games (Myerson 1991); one player + informed designer = Bayesian persuasion (KG 2011); many players + informed designer = Bergemann-Morris's own work (2013, 2016) — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 1](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- Correspondence to Forges (1993), "Five legitimate definitions of correlated equilibrium in games with incomplete information," *Theory and Decision* 35: 277-310: omniscient persuasion ↔ Forges's Bayesian solution (mediator conditions on all players' information); private persuasion ↔ communication equilibrium (mediator conditions only on truthfully reported information); public persuasion ↔ strategic-form correlated equilibrium (mediator cannot condition on players' information). BCE "generalizes omniscient persuasion by allowing many players and generalizes the Bayesian solution by allowing the information designer to have information that the players do not collectively possess" — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 3](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- Informed receiver, three regimes (bank-run example with a private signal of precision q>1/2): *omniscient persuasion* (designer knows the receiver's signal) gives Proposition 1: (γ_B, γ_G) is attainable iff γ_B ≥ max{q(1+y),1} − y + yγ_G, converging to the complete-information outcome as q→1; *private persuasion* (designer must elicit the signal; truth-telling, obedience and double-deviation constraints; the term is from Kolotilin, Li, Mylovanov & Zapechelnyuk 2015) gives a strictly smaller set; *public persuasion* (designer cannot elicit) — in this example attains everything private persuasion does, and KLMZ give sufficient conditions for the equivalence in general — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 2.2](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- Monotonicity: "the set of attainable outcomes shrinks as the receiver becomes more informed"; fixing a state space, the set shrinks in all decision problems iff information increases in Blackwell's order, and the many-player generalisation (the right notion of "more information" for many players is identified in BM 2016a) is tight — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 3](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- Public vs private signals: "If the information designer would like the players' actions to be correlated, then he will choose to give them public signals, whereas if the designer wants the players' actions to be uncorrelated, then he will choose private signals." In the 2×2×2 example (appendix of BM 2016a) public signals are best under strategic complementarities (many-player bank run) and private signals under strategic substitutes; in oligopoly information sharing (BM 2013) the optimum has firms observe conditionally independent noisy demand signals; in Ely's dynamic two-player model the designer minimises the probability both run and so chooses signals as uncorrelated as possible — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 3](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- Bergemann & Morris (2019), "Information Design: A Unified Perspective," *JEL* 57(1): 44-95 (DOI 10.1257/jel.20181489): abstract distinguishes a *literal* interpretation (a real designer commits to an information structure) from a *metaphorical* one (the analyst uses information design to characterise play across all information structures), and argues both unify communication in games, Bayesian persuasion and the authors' robust-predictions work; JEL codes C70, D82, D83 — [AEA JEL page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.20181489). Kamenica cites it as the survey that "focuses on this [multiple-receiver] case" — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf). Working-paper versions: Cowles DP 2075 (2017), 2075R-R3 (2017-18), CEPR DP 11867 — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cwl/cwldpp/2075r.html)
- Bergemann & Morris (2016b), "Information Design, Bayesian Persuasion, and Bayes Correlated Equilibrium," *AER Papers & Proceedings* 106(5): 586-591 — [Kamenica 2019 refs](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf); the authors say they "follow Taneva (2015) in our use of the term 'information design'" — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, fn. 1](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- Kamenica's classification of multi-receiver cases: two easy classes — public signals only (reinterpret â(μ) as the equilibrium action vector at common posterior μ) and private signals with own-action-only concerns and separable sender utility (solve receiver by receiver); the hard case is private signals with strategic interaction or non-separable sender utility, which "cannot be expressed in the form equivalent to the single receiver case"; the BCE route "requires the assumption that equilibrium selection works in Sender's favor" — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.1 and fn. 50](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Taneva (2019), "Information Design," *AEJ: Microeconomics* 11(4): 151-185 (DOI 10.1257/mic.20170351; earlier Edinburgh DP 256, 2015): a designer chooses how information about an unknown state is disclosed; agents receive private signals and act; shows how to compute the designer's optimal information structure in static finite environments; fully characterises the optimum in a symmetric binary game and finds that "conditionally independent private signals are never strictly optimal" there — [AEA AEJ Micro page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mic.20170351)
- Mathevet, Perego & Taneva (2020), "On Information Design in Games," *JPE* 128(4): 1370-1404 (DOI 10.1086/705332): disclosure shapes both first-order beliefs and beliefs about others' beliefs; the paper characterises how far a designer can steer these and gives "a concave-envelope representation of optimal belief distributions that covers the single-agent result of Kamenica and Gentzkow," valid under various solution concepts and selection rules, and computes an optimal structure for an investment game under adversarial selection — [IDEAS/JPE record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/doi10.1086-705332.html). Kamenica's description of the procedure: find the optimal purely private signal for every prior μ, call its value v̂(μ), then concavify v̂ to get the optimal public signal, and combine; "unlike the approach via Bayes correlated equilibria, the method ... is applicable even when Sender is concerned that equilibrium selection may not be in his favor"; computing it requires optimising over minimal consistent distributions over belief hierarchies — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.1 and fn. 51](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Applications of BCE named by Kamenica: Bergemann, Brooks & Morris (2015), "The limits of price discrimination," *AER* 105(3): 921-957 (the surplus-profit frontier across all seller signals); Bergemann, Brooks & Morris (2017), "First-price auctions with general information structures," *Econometrica* 85(1): 107-143; BCE pins down worst cases for informationally robust mechanism design (BBM 2017b, 2018b; Brooks & Du 2018) — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 48-49](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Persuading voters: Alonso & Camara (2016b) and Kosterina (2018) restrict to public signals (voters differ in preferences vs priors); Wang (2015) IID signals; Arieli & Babichenko (2016), Bardhi & Guo (2018, TE 13:1111-1150), Chan et al. (2018) allow arbitrary private signals; Kamenica calls the result "a laundry list of results rather than a coherent picture" — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Computational complexity with many receivers: Bhaskar et al. (2016, EC) — optimal public signal in a zero-sum two-receiver game is NP-hard; Babichenko & Barman (2016) — with submodular sender utility over the set of receivers taking action 1, optimum is NP-hard but a (1−1/e)-approximation is polynomial — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 44-45](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)

### Inferences
- The field's "many receivers" layer is two-sided: BCE (Bergemann-Morris) gives the feasible set under designer-favourable selection and is the natural LP; the belief-hierarchy approach (Mathevet-Perego-Taneva) is what one must use under adversarial selection, where action recommendations stop being without loss.
- The public/private signal choice is governed by whether the designer wants correlated or independent actions, which in turn tracks strategic complements vs substitutes — the cleanest portable intuition in this part of the literature.

- The JEL 2019 full text (its formal BCE definition with initial signals, its two-player investment examples, the "literal vs metaphorical" discussion in detail) could not be read; the AEA page was readable but the PDF was not, and no open mirror was found.
- Taneva 2019's full set of results (beyond the abstract) was not verified.

## Key question 4: Standard extensions — what each adds

### Takeaway
Each extension relaxes one primitive of KG 2011. Multiple senders: with rich signal spaces equilibria are characterised by every sender's v̂ touching its concavification on the support, and competition cannot make outcomes less informative than collusion iff the environment is "Blackwell connected". Dynamics matter only when the state moves, actions constrain future actions, or timing preferences conflict; Ely's "Beeps" shows the optimal policy can be simple (reveal with a deterministic delay). Informed receivers are handled by integrating private information into v̂, and eliciting it does not help the sender in linear environments (KMZL 2017). Costly signals with posterior-separable costs keep concavification alive and bridge to rational inattention. Limited commitment (Lipnowski-Ravid-Shishkin) yields a geometric characterisation mixing concavification and quasiconcavification, with cheap talk (Lipnowski-Ravid 2020) at the zero-credibility end.

### Cited Findings

**Multiple senders.**
- Gentzkow & Kamenica (2017a), "Bayesian persuasion with multiple senders and rich signal spaces," *Games and Economic Behavior* 104: 411-429: signals are partitions of Ω×[0,1] (a formalism from Green & Stokey 1978), so the join ∨ of signals is well defined; a Bayes-plausible τ is a pure-strategy equilibrium outcome iff for every μ in supp τ and every sender i, v̂_i coincides with its concavification at μ, i.e. supp τ ⊆ ∩_i M_i — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.2](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Competition vs collusion: the collusive outcome τ^c (maximising Σ v̂_i) and an equilibrium τ* may be Blackwell-incomparable, so the receiver can prefer collusion for some u; but if comparable, τ* is more informative — "it can never be the case that the collusive outcome is strictly more informative than an equilibrium outcome" — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.2](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Gentzkow & Kamenica (2017b), "Competition in Persuasion," *Review of Economic Studies* 84(1): 300-322 (DOI 10.1093/restud/rdw052; NBER WP 17436, 2011): with general signal sets Π_i, the collusive outcome cannot be strictly more informative than any equilibrium regardless of preferences iff the informational environment is *Blackwell connected* (each sender can unilaterally deviate to induce any feasible more-informative outcome); the rich signal space is Blackwell connected; the same condition governs whether adding senders or reducing alignment must increase information; mixed strategies break Blackwell connectedness — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.2 and fn. 66](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v84y2017i1p300-322..html)
- Li & Norman (2018a, *Econ. Letters* 170:66-70; 2018b) on sequential senders: adding a first mover cannot make the equilibrium strictly less informative, but adding one in the middle or end can — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 57](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Board & Lu (2018), "Competitive information disclosure in search markets," *JPE* 126(5): 1965-2010: with rich signals and observable buyer beliefs, competition has no bite (each seller uses the monopoly signal); without perfect correlation and with unobservable beliefs, the equilibrium approaches full revelation as search cost → 0 — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.2](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Earlier non-persuasion support for "competition reveals information": Milgrom & Roberts (1986, RAND), Shin (1998, RAND), Battaglini (2002, *Econometrica*); Gentzkow & Shapiro (2008, JEP) — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.2](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)

**Dynamic persuasion.**
- When the state and action set are fixed and players are patient, dynamics are irrelevant: any sequence of signals induces some Bayes-plausible τ, so the sender might as well induce it at once. Dynamics matter when the state evolves, past behaviour constrains current opportunities, or sender and receiver disagree about timing — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.3](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Ely (2017), "Beeps," *AER* 107(1): 31-53 (DOI 10.1257/aer.20150218): optimal information policy with a Markov state and a myopic receiver. Promotion example: state switches 0→1 at Poisson rate λ, worker asks iff belief ≥ p, firm always prefers no request; no information → belief 1−e^{−λt} and request at t=−ln(1−p)/λ; full transparency → request when ready (mean 1/λ); neither is ever optimal — the optimum reveals readiness with a deterministic delay of −ln(1−p)/λ. Ely also extends to multiple audiences where higher-order beliefs matter and derives bank-run-reducing policies — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.3 and fn. 25, 73](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf); [AEA DOI page](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20161441) (search confirmation of volume/pages)
- Renault, Solan & Vieille (2017), "Optimal dynamic information provision," *GEB* 104: 329-349 (TSE WP 17-749): hidden Markov state, advisor with commitment steers a decision maker's choice between risky and safe actions; a "greedy" rule that reveals as little as possible each period while maximising the current payoff is optimal in many but not all cases — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/gamebe/v104y2017icp329-349.html); Kamenica: "Renault et al. (2017) also analyze a case of this model" — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 72](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Ely & Szydlowski (2019/2020), "Moving the goalposts," *JPE*: fixed state, effort-based promotion; dynamics matter because future information is an incentive for current effort and sunk effort makes further information induce continuation; dynamic provision beats static — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.3](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Ely, Frankel & Kamenica (2015), "Suspense and surprise," *JPE* 123(1): 215-260 (cited in the survey's reference list with a volume typo "103"): belief dynamics enter utility directly — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 71](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Other dynamic work named: Hörner & Skrzypacz (2016), "Selling information," *JPE* 124(6); Henry & Ottaviani (2019), "Research and approval process," *AER* 109:911-955; sequentially-rational (no-commitment) dynamic senders in Orlov, Skrzypacz & Zryumov (2018a) and Bizzotto, Rüdiger & Vigier (2018) — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 70, 75](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)

**Privately informed receiver.**
- KG 2011 Sec. VI.A: private information about the state or preferences is absorbed into v̂ by integrating over the receiver's type; Props 1, Cors 1-2 survive, but private information "smooths out" v̂ so the indifference property (Prop 5) can fail — [KG 2011, Sec. VI.A](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Kolotilin, Mylovanov, Zapechelnyuk & Li (2017), "Persuasion of a privately informed receiver," *Econometrica* 85(6): 1949-1964 (DOI 10.3982/ECTA13251): linear environments, binary action, receiver with a private type; a *persuasion mechanism* conditions disclosure on the reported type, an *experiment* does not; they "establish the equivalence of implementation by persuasion mechanisms and by experiments, and characterize optimal persuasion mechanisms"; the supplement shows that with more than two actions mechanisms can do strictly better — [Econometrica page](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2017/11/01/persuasion-privately-informed-receiver). Kamenica: "Kolotilin et al. (2017) show that Sender does not benefit from the ability to elicit Receiver's private information"; Li & Shi (2017, *AER* 107(11): 3363-3385) show discriminatory disclosure dominates full disclosure when a seller conditions on reports — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 30](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Guo & Shmaya (2017/2019), "The interval structure of optimal disclosure": with binary action and receiver private information about the state the optimum is a "nested interval" structure — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 23](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Matyskova (2018): receiver can gather costly information after the signal; WLOG she never does on path; the threat weakly harms the sender — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Heterogeneous priors: Alonso & Camara (2016a), "Bayesian persuasion with heterogeneous priors," *JET* 165: 672-706: as long as the receiver's action depends only on her posterior mean, the sender generically benefits from persuasion even when the receiver is mistaken in his favour — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)

**Costly signals and rational inattention.**
- Gentzkow & Kamenica (2014), "Costly persuasion," *AER P&P* 104(5): 457-462: sender payoff v(a,ω) − c(π); "Bayesian persuasion assumes c(π)=0, while rational inattention assumes u=v"; if c is posterior separable, c(π)=E_{μ~⟨π⟩}[H(μ0) − H(μ)], the problem becomes max_τ E_τ[v̂(μ)+H(μ)] s.t. Bayes plausibility, so concavification still works; the benchmark belief need not be μ0; posterior-separable costs are monotone in Blackwell order iff H is concave — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4 and fn. 34-36](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf); abstract confirmed: the price of a signal scales with how much it reduces uncertainty, and the concavification method still applies — [EconBiz record](https://econbiz.de/Record/costly-persuasion-gentzkow-matthew-aaron/10010390886)
- Restricted signal sets as a special (non-monotone) cost: Brocas & Carrillo (2007); Ichihashi (2017) on which restriction of the sender's set maximises receiver welfare (fully characterised for binary actions); Tsakas & Tsakas (2018) exogenous noise; Perez-Richet & Skreta (2018) test design under falsification — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 34](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)

**Limited commitment and the cheap-talk end.**
- Min (2017 WP; 2021), "Bayesian persuasion under partial commitment," *Economic Theory* 72(3): 743-764: with partial commitment the sender's self-interest erodes the Pareto-optimal welfare of full commitment, but can still beat the best cheap-talk welfare via a truth-telling-based or a pure-noise-based structure — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/joecth/v72y2021i3d10.1007_s00199-021-01386-1.html); Kamenica: Min shows commitment helps both sender and receiver in the Crawford-Sobel uniform-quadratic setting — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 40](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Lipnowski, Ravid & Shishkin (2018 WP; 2022), "Persuasion via Weak Institutions," *JPE* 130(10): 2705-2730: after publicly choosing the signal, the sender can alter the realization with some probability (1 − credibility). With state-independent sender preferences, a geometric approach similar to concavification characterises the equilibrium — the sender's best payoff is "the concave envelope of a capped value function"; the receiver can be better off when the sender is less credible; there is generically a credibility level at which the sender's payoff jumps, but under the assumption that the sender need not rule out any state to get his preferred action, full credibility is not such a threshold (small loss of credibility → small loss) — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4 and fn. 41-43](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf); [SSRN 3168103](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3168103); [JPE page](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/epdfplus/10.1086/720462)
- Lipnowski & Ravid (2017 WP "Cheap talk with a fixed agenda"; 2020), "Cheap Talk with Transparent Motives," *Econometrica* 88(4): 1631-1660 (DOI 10.3982/ECTA15674): pure cheap talk with state-independent sender preferences; the sender's maximal equilibrium payoff is the *quasiconcavification* (smallest quasiconcave function above v̂) of his value function at the prior; the sender "gains credibility by degrading self-serving information" — [Econometrica page](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2020/07/01/cheap-talk-transparent-motives); [Kamenica 2019, fn. 41](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf). LRS show the limited-commitment payoff is "an object that combines the concavification and the quasiconcavification. (Alas, it is not a simple convex combination of the two.)" — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 41](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Frechette, Lizzeri & Perego (2017, later *Econometrica*), "Rules and commitment in communication": the same partial-commitment model, tested in the lab — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 40](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Privately informed sender (signal chosen after seeing information): Perez-Richet (2014), "Interim Bayesian persuasion: first steps," *AER P&P* 104(5): 469-474; Hedlund (2017), "Bayesian persuasion by a privately informed sender," *JET* 167: 229-268 — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 38](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Reputation as a commitment substitute: Best & Quigley (2017), "Persuasion for the long run" — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 2.2](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)

### Inferences
- The extensions line up on one axis — how much of the KG commitment-plus-rich-signal-space assumption survives: full commitment (KG), posterior-separable cost (still concavification), partial credibility (concave envelope of a capped function), zero credibility (quasiconcavification). This makes the Lipnowski-Ravid quasiconcavification the natural "cheap-talk limit" of persuasion for state-independent senders.
- For dynamics, the clean rule "dynamics matter only if state moves / actions constrain / timing conflicts" is the right first filter for a newcomer.

- Ely (2017)'s formal characterisation beyond the promotion example (general Markov-state optimum) was not read in the original; the search returned only the abstract.
- Lipnowski-Ravid-Shishkin's exact "capped value function" definition and the Lipnowski-Ravid theorem statements (including upper-semicontinuity conditions) were not verified against the published texts.
- The Econometrica publication details of Frechette-Lizzeri-Perego were not confirmed.

## Key question 5: Formal relationship to mechanism design and to cheap talk

### Takeaway
Information design is the dual of mechanism design: the mechanism designer fixes the game with the information given, the information designer fixes the information with the game given, and each has a revelation principle (direct mechanisms with type sets = action sets; recommendation signals with type sets = action sets). Relative to cheap talk, verifiable disclosure and signalling, Bayesian persuasion gives the sender maximal commitment, which makes the problem non-strategic, kills babbling, and bounds from above the gains any communication protocol can deliver.

### Cited Findings
- Kamenica: "In mechanism design problems, the allocation of information (i.e., who knows what) is given, and the designer influences the outcome by selecting the game that the agents will play. In information design problems, the game that the agents play is given, and the designer influences the outcome by specifying the allocation of information" (citing Bergemann & Morris 2016b, Taneva 2016) — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Bergemann & Morris: a mechanism is "the set of players, their available actions and a mapping from action profiles to outcomes"; the information designer "has no ability to change the mechanism (or force the players to make particular action choices)", the mechanism designer "has no ability to choose the information structure"; in each case a revelation-principle argument lets the analyst consider all mechanisms / all information structures — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 1](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- KG 2011 already notes the (i)-(ii) equivalence of Proposition 1 is "closely analogous to the revelation principle (e.g., Myerson 1979), except that the revelation principle applies to problems where players' information is a given, while our problem is that of designing the informational environment" — [KG 2011, p. 2595](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Limits of the analogy: just as the revelation principle fails in mechanism design without designer-favourable selection (Maskin 1999), recommendation signals no longer suffice with multiple receivers and adversarial selection — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 5](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- BCE = information design: "the set of Bayes correlated equilibria, by definition, also coincides with the set of outcomes that can be attained through information design. Thus, a Bayesian persuasion problem is equivalent to a problem of selecting an optimal Bayes correlated equilibrium given an objective function" — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Combining instruments: Li (2017, *Econ. Letters* 161:93-95) adds transfers to the 2×2 persuasion model; Lewis & Sappington (1994, *IER* 35(2)) a monopolist choosing price and information; Bergemann, Bonatti & Smolin (2018), "The design and price of information," *AER* 108(1): 1-48, and the Bergemann & Bonatti (2019) *Annual Review* survey cover markets where information is sold rather than used to persuade — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 2 and Sec. 1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Cheap talk and other protocols: Bayesian persuasion is "a communication protocol, in the tradition of cheap talk (Crawford & Sobel 1982), verifiable message (Grossman 1981, Milgrom 1981), and signaling games (Spence 1973). Relative to these other models of communication, Bayesian persuasion endows the sender with more commitment power" — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- KG 2011 on why commitment matters: it "makes our environment effectively nonstrategic. Receiver solves a simple decision problem ... Sender's choice of a signal establishes the meaning of messages and eliminates the possibility of babbling equilibria"; Crawford-Sobel (1982, *Econometrica* 50(6): 1431-1451) — costless messages regardless of knowledge; Grossman (1981, *J. Law Econ.*) and Milgrom (1981, *Bell J. Econ.*) — truth but not the whole truth; Kartik (2009, *REStud* 76(4)) — lying costs; Spence (1973, *QJE*) — costly signals — [KG 2011, Sec. I.C](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Equivalence results: full commitment ≡ sender publicly chooses how much to learn, then discloses verifiably (Gentzkow & Kamenica 2017c, "Disclosure of endogenous information," *Econ. Theory Bull.* 5(1): 47-56; also KG 2011 online appendix) — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 1](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Upper bound: the sender's gain under commitment weakly exceeds his gain in any alternative communication game (KG 2011 online appendix) — [KG 2011, Sec. I.C](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Contrast with delegation (receiver commits, sender does not: Holmström 1984, Dessein 2002, Alonso & Matouschek 2008; Melumad & Shibano 1991): there communication depends on both bias parameters α and ω*, while in persuasion only α matters; with a sender-worst action and two-sided commitment, full disclosure is always an equilibrium — [KG 2011, fn. 17](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Cheap talk with state-independent preferences is exactly the zero-credibility limit: quasiconcavification (Lipnowski-Ravid 2020) vs concavification (KG 2011), with the limited-commitment case in between (Lipnowski-Ravid-Shishkin) — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 41](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Behavioural link: the belief-based reformulation connects to models with explicit preferences over beliefs (Caplin & Leahy 2004; Kőszegi 2006; Eliaz & Spiegler 2006) — [KG 2011, fn. 6](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)

### Inferences
- The cleanest formal statement for a newcomer: mechanism design = choose the game, information fixed, revelation principle over types; information design = choose the information, game fixed, revelation principle over actions (obedience); BCE is the feasible set of the latter the way incentive-compatible direct mechanisms are the feasible set of the former.
- The cheap-talk comparison is best organised by credibility level rather than as a separate literature.

### Gaps
- Bergemann & Morris 2019's own formal exposition of the duality (and of the "metaphorical" use for robust predictions) could not be read; the above reconstructs it from the 2016 note and Kamenica's survey.

- Grading interpretation: a school's grading policy maps ability ω to a (possibly random) grade; the labour market is the receiver; the school maximises its average valuation of placements; the stochastic map can reflect orthogonal attributes like attendance — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 2.2](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf); KG 2011 lists "a school choosing a coarse grading policy" and "a rating agency choosing a scoring procedure for hospitals" as settings where the sender learns everything but commits to a disclosure rule — [KG 2011, Sec. I.C](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Law enforcement (Lazear 2006): Z miles of road, G<Z police, speeding utility V, fine K>V; the state is presence of a policeman on a mile (prior G/Z), the signal is the predictability of patrol locations; random patrols = uninformative signal (speed everywhere iff V > GK/Z), fixed patrols = fully informative; when V > GK/Z neither extreme is optimal and partial predictability is best — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 2.2](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Stress tests: Goldstein & Leitner (2018), "Stress Tests and Information Disclosure," *Journal of Economic Theory* 177: 34-69 (DOI 10.1016/j.jet.2018.05.013; Philadelphia Fed WP 13-26/15-10/17-28): a regulator learns banks' health and decides what to disclose; disclosure can destroy risk-sharing among banks (Hirshleifer effect) yet some disclosure is needed in bad times; the optimal rule is sometimes simple (full, none, cutoff) and sometimes has multiple cutoffs or is non-monotone; keywords include "Bayesian persuasion" — [WUSTL profile record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/stress-tests-and-information-disclosure/); [Philadelphia Fed WP 17-28R](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/FRBP/Assets/working-papers/2017/wp17-28r.pdf?sc_lang=en). Note: Kamenica's reference list prints the volume as "117"; the publisher record says 177. The Bergemann-Morris bank-run example is itself read as "a regulator running stress tests to obfuscate the state of a bank in order to prevent a run" — [Bergemann & Morris 2016, Sec. 2.1](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2027.pdf)
- Inostroza & Pavan, "Persuasion in global games with application to stress testing": robust (adversarial-selection) information design in global games; the optimal policy coordinates all market participants on the same action and the paper identifies when it takes a pass/fail form — [Kamenica 2019 refs](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf) (content as reported in search results; the paper itself was not read)
- Political persuasion: Alonso & Câmara (2016), "Persuading Voters," *AER* 106(11): 3590-3605 (DOI 10.1257/aer.20140737): a politician designs a public experiment; under a non-unanimous rule she targets realizations at different winning coalitions, so "under a simple-majority rule, a majority of voters might be strictly worse off"; the paper also asks which voting rules voters prefer and when a majority would favour supermajority or unanimity to extract a more informative experiment — [IDEAS/AER record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v106y2016i11p3590-3605.html)
- Routing: Das, Kamenica & Mirka (2017), "Reducing Congestion Through Information Design," *Proc. 55th Allerton Conference*, pp. 1279-1284: in a two-path example with an uncertain path, sending each driver a suitable independent signal brings traffic on the congestion-prone path down to the social optimum — [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8262884); [author PDF](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~rmirka/reducing-congestion-information-2.pdf); Kamenica adds that a monopolist routing provider could raise everyone's welfare with partial information and that congestion games are "fertile ground" — [Kamenica 2019, fn. 52](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- Media / competition in the market for ideas: the multi-sender results are motivated by freedom-of-press, media-ownership and adversarial-procedure policy (Gentzkow & Kamenica 2017b; Gentzkow & Shapiro 2008); Levy, Moreno de Barreda & Razin (2018, CEPR DP 12640) show a multi-outlet owner can exploit correlation neglect — [Kamenica 2019, Sec. 4.2 and fn. 69](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2019-kamenica.pdf)
- KG 2011's own applications: lobbying (full or no study, never a biased one, for a rational politician) and product information / free trials in a Lewis-Sappington-style model where the consumer buys iff E_μ ω ≥ outside option — [KG 2011, Sec. V](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)
- Real-world commitment settings listed by KG 2011: *Brady v. Maryland* (373 US 83, 1963) forces prosecutors to disclose exculpatory evidence; registered clinical trials and FDA-approved trial designs; tobacco-funded third-party studies; public taste tests and software trial versions — [KG 2011, Sec. I.C](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf)

### Inferences
- The applied literature splits into "sender is a player" (prosecutor, lobbyist, firm, politician) and "sender is a planner" (regulator, routing platform, school), matching Kamenica's remark that "Bayesian persuasion" is used for the former and "information design" for the latter.

### Gaps
- Boleslavsky & Cotton (2015) and Ostrovsky & Schwarz (2010) could not be fetched; they are cited only through Kamenica's survey and KG 2011, with no independent verification of their results.
- Inostroza & Pavan's publication venue after 2018 was not confirmed (it falls on the boundary of the 2021 cutoff).
