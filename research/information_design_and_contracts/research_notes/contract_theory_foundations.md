## Q1. What are the main branches, and what distinguishes each?

### Takeaway
The field splits by *what the principal cannot see or enforce*: hidden action (moral hazard), hidden information (adverse selection / screening, and its mirror, signalling), unverifiable-but-observable states (incomplete contracts, relational contracts), and the time dimension (dynamic contracts, career concerns). The 2016 Nobel press release frames the two founding strands as Holmström's optimal incentive design under partly unobserved action and Hart's incomplete contracts and allocation of control rights.

### Cited Findings
- The 2016 prize to Hart and Holmström was "for their contributions to contract theory"; Holmström "demonstrated how a principal should design an optimal contract for an agent, whose action is partly unobserved by the principal," with his "informativeness principle" stating "precisely how this contract should link the agent's pay to performance-relevant information"; in the 1980s Hart "made fundamental contributions to a new branch of contract theory that deals with the important case of incomplete contracts" and "which party should have control rights over the contract" — [Nobel press release 2016](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2016/press-release/)
- Schmidt's survey of the prize organises the Holmström strand as: the basic moral hazard model and informativeness principle; linear contracts (Holmström-Milgrom 1987); multitasking (Holmström-Milgrom 1991, 1994); moral hazard in teams (Holmström 1982a); career concerns (Holmström 1982b/1999); and the Hart strand as: residual control rights and property rights (Grossman-Hart 1986, Hart-Moore 1990), corporate finance (Hart-Moore 1998), privatisation (Hart-Shleifer-Vishny 1997), the foundations debate (Maskin-Tirole 1999, Hart-Moore 1999), and contracts as reference points (Hart-Moore 2008) — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Schmidt: "The work of Bengt Holmström and Oliver Hart is conceptually quite different, but they both start out from the same problem," namely that the informativeness principle "implies that the shape of the optimal incentive scheme in a standard moral hazard problem is incredibly complex and highly sensitive to small changes in the (unobservable) beliefs of the contracting parties" and "yields no robust predictions on the shape of the optimal incentive scheme." He quotes Hart and Holmström's 1987 World Congress survey: "optimal contracts become monstrous state-contingent prescriptions (p. 74)" — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Laffont and Martimort's textbook (Princeton UP, 2002) organises the single-principal field into chapters on adverse selection, moral hazard, nonverifiability, mixed models, and dynamics under full commitment — [oeaw.ac.at record of The Theory of Incentives](https://oeaw.ac.at/resources/Record/9781400829453)
- Salanié's primer "covers mainly adverse selection, signaling, and moral hazard," with chapter 7 on incomplete contracts and chapter 8 on the empirical literature rewritten for the 2005 second edition — [Salanié, The Economics of Contracts, 2nd ed. listing](https://www.tenlong.com.tw/products/9780262534222)
- Relational contracts branch: MacLeod and Malcomson (Econometrica 57(2), 1989) study "the enforceability of employment contracts when employees' performance cannot be verified in court," modelling self-enforcing implicit contracts "as perfect equilibria of a repeated game" — [Econometric Society page](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1989/03/01/implicit-contracts-incentive-compatibility-and-involuntary)
- Levin (AER 93(3), 2003): "Standard incentive theory models ... assume that contracts can be perfectly enforced. This paper studies the design of self-enforced relational contracts." — [IDEAS record of Levin 2003](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v93y2003i3p835-857.html)
- Signalling branch: Spence, "Job Market Signaling," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 87(3), Aug. 1973, pp. 355-374 — [Spence 1973 PDF](https://cies.org.pe/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/spence_1973.pdf)
- Career concerns as implicit contracts: Prendergast describes career concerns as arising "in situations where agents exert effort not just to maximize current pay but also to affect the perceptions of others," following "Eugene Fama (1980) and Holmstrom (1982)" — [Prendergast 1999](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1999-prendergast.pdf)

### Inferences
- A clean taxonomy for a newcomer: (1) moral hazard = hidden action after contracting; (2) adverse selection/screening = hidden type before contracting, uninformed party moves first; (3) signalling = same hidden type, informed party moves first; (4) incomplete contracts = states observable to both parties but not verifiable by courts, so ownership/control rights are the contractual instrument; (5) relational contracts = unverifiable performance enforced by the threat of ending a repeated relationship; (6) dynamic contracts = repeated moral hazard or adverse selection with memory, savings, renegotiation and commitment issues; (7) career concerns = the market, not a contract, supplies incentives.
- The intellectual hinge of the whole field, per Schmidt, is the "complexity problem" of the informativeness principle: branches (linear contracts, multitasking, incomplete contracts, robust contracts) can each be read as an answer to why real contracts are simpler than the 1979 theory predicts.

## Q2. Moral hazard (hidden action): Mirrlees, Holmström 1979, Grossman-Hart 1983, Holmström-Milgrom 1987, first-order approach, limited liability

### Takeaway
Holmström 1979 shows the optimal contract rewards every signal that is statistically informative about effort (the sufficient-statistic / informativeness principle); Mirrlees showed the problem can fail to have a solution and that the first-best can be approximated by extreme penalties; Grossman-Hart 1983 made the problem rigorous with a two-step discrete method and showed the optimal contract has almost no general shape; Rogerson 1985 and Jewitt 1988 give conditions validating the first-order approach; Holmström-Milgrom 1987 showed that with a rich (continuous-time, Brownian) action space the optimal contract is linear in aggregates.

### Cited Findings
- Holmström, "Moral Hazard and Observability," *Bell Journal of Economics* 10(1), Spring 1979, pp. 74-91. Its abstract gives "a necessary and sufficient condition for imperfect information to improve on contracts based on the payoff alone" and characterises how such information should be used — [Holmström 1979 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/1979-holmstrom.pdf)
- Sufficient-statistic form, from a lecture summary: "Holmstrom (1979) shows that y has no incremental value if and only if x is a sufficient statistic for x and y given a" (signal y, payoff x, action a) — [Duke lecture note Module 3.3](https://sites.duke.edu/xjiang/files/2025/06/Module3_3.pdf)
- Schmidt: the principle "requires that the wage of the agent should be conditional on all observable signals that contain statistical information about the agent's effort"; this implies relative performance evaluation (Holmström 1982a); and it "requires that the principal rewards the agent as if she solves a statistical inference problem ... However, in equilibrium the agent chooses the desired action with probability one!" The optimal wage is governed by the likelihood ratio: if a higher profit is associated with a lower likelihood ratio "indicating that it is 'less likely' that this profit level was generated by the desired action, then the wage of the agent should decrease" — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Prendergast's statement: "Perhaps the most important observation of the early contributions to agency theory (Holmstrom 1979) is what has become known as the Informativeness Principle, which (loosely) implies that any measure of performance that (on the margin) reveals information on the effort level chosen by the agent should be included in the compensation contract." Its most common application is Relative Performance Evaluation — [Prendergast 1999](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1999-prendergast.pdf)
- Schmidt notes "A similar model was developed independently by Shavell (1979). Mirrlees (1975) worked on the principal-agent problem" earlier — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- The "Mirrlees problem": "Mirrlees (1975) has shown that a solution to the moral hazard problem need not exist if the agent chooses the mean of an unbounded probability distribution (such as the normal distribution). Furthermore, he shows in a simple example that the principal can approximate the first best by offering a contract that pays the agent a fixed wage for almost all realizations of profit, but penalizes him heavily if the firm's profit falls below a very low threshold." — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- The 1975 manuscript was finally published as Mirrlees, "The Theory of Moral Hazard and Unobservable Behaviour: Part I," *Review of Economic Studies* 66(1), 1999, pp. 3-21: "For a model with bounded agent's utility, conditions are given under which the first-best equilibrium can be approximated arbitrarily closely by contracts relating payment to observable outcomes," and "for general models, the solution may not always be obtained by using the agent's first-order conditions as constraint" — [IDEAS record of Mirrlees 1999](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/oup/restud/v66y1999i1p3-21..html)
- Grossman and Hart, "An Analysis of the Principal-Agent Problem," *Econometrica* 51(1), Jan. 1983, pp. 7-45 — [Kóczy & Kiss 2017 reference list](https://hitelintezetiszemle.mnb.hu/en/laszlo-a-koczy-hubert-janos-kiss). Schmidt: it "offer[s] a discrete model with finitely many actions and outcomes that avoids these technical problems. It proposes a two-step solution algorithm. First, for every possible action ... the cost-minimizing incentive scheme is computed that implements this action at the lowest possible cost ... In a second step the action that maximizes the principal's profit (given the cost to implement it) is selected." Also: "the optimal contract has very few general properties. It need not even be the case that the optimal contract is monotonically increasing" — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Schmidt states the informativeness principle was "first established by Holmström (1979) and refined and stated more rigorously by Grossman and Hart (1983)" — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- First-order approach. Rogerson, "The First-Order Approach to Principal-Agent Problems," *Econometrica* 53(6), Nov. 1985, pp. 1357-1368: sufficient conditions are the monotone likelihood ratio condition and the convexity of the distribution function condition; under them the Pareto-optimal wage contract is nondecreasing in output — [Econometric Society page for Rogerson 1985](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1985/11/01/first-order-approach-principal-agent-problems)
- Jewitt, "Justifying the First-Order Approach to Principal-Agent Problems," *Econometrica* 56(5), Sept. 1988, pp. 1177-1190: "the Mirrlees-Rogerson conditions do not work if the principal can observe more than one relevant statistic," and the convexity assumption "is satisfied by a very few of the distributions occurring in statistics textbooks"; gives alternative conditions including the multi-statistic case — [Econometric Society page for Jewitt 1988](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1988/09/01/justifying-first-order-approach-principal-agent-problems)
- Schmidt on why the FOA is problematic: "the first order condition is only a necessary but not a sufficient condition for the optimal action of the agent. Whether the agent's maximization problem is globally concave depends on the incentive scheme that is determined endogenously." — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Holmström and Milgrom, "Aggregation and Linearity in the Provision of Intertemporal Incentives," *Econometrica* 55(2), March 1987, pp. 303-328: "the optimal compensation scheme takes the form of a linear function of a vector of N accounts that count how often each observable event occurs"; when the principal only sees coarser totals, the best scheme is linear in those totals; linearity is tied to the agent's wide range of possible actions; assumes exponential utility and normal distributions — [Econometric Society page for HM 1987](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1987/03/01/aggregation-and-linearity-provision-intertemporal-incentives)
- Schmidt's gloss: "In the limit, as the length of each time period goes to zero, the agent controls the drift rate of an N-dimensional Brownian motion in continuous time ... the optimal incentive scheme in the continuous model is a contract that is linear in aggregates, e.g. linear in total profits. The intuition for this result is that any non-linear incentive scheme would be exploited by the agent." The model "predicts that the incentive intensity (the slope of the incentive scheme) is higher the lower the agent's risk aversion, the smaller the noise term, and the higher the agent's impact on profits." Footnote: "In Holmström and Milgrom there is a discontinuity in the limit ... Hellwig and Schmidt (2002) show how this discontinuity arises" — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Prendergast's reduced-form CARA-normal version: with signals y = e + α + ε_y and s = e + α + ε_s, "following Holmstrom and Milgrom (1987), the optimal contract relating wages to these observed signals is linear ... w = β0 + βy y + βs s" — [Prendergast 1999](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1999-prendergast.pdf)
- The single-task linear-contract formula appears explicitly in Holmström-Milgrom 1991 as the independent-tasks special case: "α_i = B_i / (1 + r C_ii σ_i^2) for all i ... α_i is decreasing in risk aversion (r) and risk (σ_i^2). It is also decreasing in C_ii ... α_i should be higher, the more responsive the agent is to incentives" (notation reconstructed from an OCR'd scan; B_i is the marginal benefit, C the agent's cost function) — [HM 1991 PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Limited liability: Innes, "Limited Liability and Incentive Contracting with Ex Ante Action Choices," *Journal of Economic Theory* 52(1), 1990 — [UF reading list (Sappington)](https://economics.clas.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/117/ECO7113_InformationEconomics_SAPPINGTON_SP19.pdf). Course material: "with limited liability this is no longer possible, since they can't pay the agent a negative wage. As a result, the first best is no longer achievable" — [Aalto course material](https://mycourses.aalto.fi/mod/resource/view.php?id=1188607)
- Schmidt on the risk-neutral benchmark: with a risk-neutral agent the first best is reached by selling the firm to the agent, but "the agent may be unable to buy the firm because he is cash constrained" (footnote 4) — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Empirical caveat on the risk-incentive trade-off: "Some studies find stronger incentives when performance measurement is easier (e.g. Aggarwal and Samwick, 1999), while other studies find a positive relationship or no correlation at all (e.g. Core, Guay, and Verrecchia, 2003, Lafontaine, 1992, Allen and Lueck, 1992)" — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)

### Inferences
- The canonical intuition chain: the principal pays as a Bayesian statistician inferring effort from output (likelihood-ratio contract) → this makes contracts depend on every informative signal and on fine distributional detail → the field's later work (linear contracts, multitasking, robustness) is a search for simplicity and robustness that the 1979 theory lacks.
- The first-order approach is what makes the Holmström 1979 characterisation tractable; MLRP+CDFC (Rogerson) or Jewitt's alternatives are the standard conditions quoted in textbooks.

- Innes 1990's specific result (the optimal contract under limited liability and monotonicity is a debt-like "live-or-die" contract) was not confirmed from a source in this session; only the citation was verified.
- Shavell 1979 (Bell Journal) was only seen named in Schmidt's footnote; venue not independently verified.

## Q3. Multitasking: Holmström-Milgrom 1991, Baker 1992, Baker-Gibbons-Murphy 1994 (the Goodhart branch)

### Takeaway
Holmström-Milgrom 1991 (JLEO 7, special issue, pp. 24-52) proves that when an agent divides attention across tasks and some are poorly measured, incentives on the measurable task pull effort away from the unmeasured one, so the optimal incentive on any task *falls* with the measurement difficulty of competing tasks and can be exactly zero (a fixed wage) even with a perfect measure and a risk-neutral agent; Baker 1992 shows that even with risk neutrality, incentives are muted below first-best when the performance measure is misaligned with the principal's objective; Baker-Gibbons-Murphy 1994 show subjective (relational) measures and objective measures can be substitutes or complements.

### Cited Findings — Holmström & Milgrom 1991
- Bibliographic: Holmström and Milgrom, "Multitask Principal-Agent Analyses: Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design," *Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization* 7, Special Issue, 1991, pp. 24-52, JSTOR stable 764957 — [HM 1991 PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Motivation: "It remains a puzzle for this theory that employment contracts so often specify fixed wages and more generally that incentives within firms appear to be so muted, especially compared to those of the market." The model "(i) can account for paying fixed wages even when good, objective output measures are available and agents are highly responsive to incentive pay; (ii) can make recommendations and predictions about ownership patterns even when contracts can take full account of all observable variables and court enforcement is perfect; (iii) can explain why employment is sometimes superior to independent contracting ...; (iv) can explain bureaucratic constraints; and (v) can shed light on how tasks get allocated to different jobs." — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- The teacher example: opponents of test-based pay argue "teachers would sacrifice such activities as promoting curiosity and creative thinking ... in order to teach the narrowly defined basic skills that are tested on standardized exams. It would be better, these critics argue, to pay a fixed wage without any incentive scheme." Footnote 3 cites a 1989 Greenville, SC teacher caught passing test answers to raise her performance rating — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- The two fundamental differences from one-task models: "when there are multiple tasks, incentive pay serves not only to allocate risks and to motivate hard work, it also serves to direct the allocation of the agents' attention among their various duties"; and "job design is an important instrument for the control of incentives" — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Model: "our linear principal-agent model (Holmstrom and Milgrom, 1987), mainly specialized to the case where the agent's costs depend only on the total effort or attention the agent devotes to all of his tasks. This modeling assures that an increase in an agent's compensation in any one task will cause some reallocation of attention away from other tasks." — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- The headline result, stated by the authors: "First, we show that an optimal incentive contract can be to pay a fixed wage independent of measured performance ... More generally, the desirability of providing incentives for any one activity decreases with the difficulty of measuring performance in any other activities that make competing demands on the agent's time and attention. This result may explain a substantial part of the puzzle of why incentive clauses are so much less common than one-dimensional theories would predict." — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Precise two-task formula (task 2 = unmeasurable, σ_2 = ∞): if the optimum has t > 0 it satisfies "α_1 = (B_1 − B_2 C_12/C_22) / [1 + r σ_1^2 (C_11 − C_12^2/C_22)]" (eq. 7; reconstructed from OCR). "When C_12 is negative [complements], making it more negative leads to a larger optimal value of α_1 ... If the two dimensions of teaching are substitutes in the agent's cost function (C_12 > 0), then α_1 is correspondingly reduced, because high values of α_1 cause the teacher to substitute effort away from teaching higher-thinking skills." — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Two routes to zero incentives even with perfect measurement: (a) "(7) allows the possibility that it may be optimal to set α_1 negative even if B_1 is positive, provided B_1 < B_2 C_12/C_22. If the agent can always reduce measured performance at no cost to himself, then this observation can be used to produce robust examples in which it is optimal to provide zero incentives for a desirable activity even when perfectly reliable performance measures (σ^2 = 0) may exist"; (b) "when effort in the two activities are perfect substitutes in the agent's cost function and the second activity is unobservable ... the incentive constraint ... implies that α_1 = α_2 (intuitively, the agent must equate the marginal return to effort in various tasks). If ... σ_2 = ∞, it then follows that 0 = α_2 = α_1." — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- The general principle: "when inputs are substitutes, incentives for any given activity t_i can be provided either by rewarding that activity or by reducing its opportunity cost (by reducing the incentives for the other activities). Here, t_2 cannot be measured at all, so the only way to provide incentives for t_2 is to reduce α_1." — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Fixed cost of incentives: "Providing incentives for an activity involves an inherent fixed cost" because any positive incentive imposes a risk premium of at least ½ r [C'(0)]² σ², whereas zero incentive imposes none; footnote 9: "In multitask models ... the fact that agents supply inputs even without incentive pay can be quite consequential" — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Proposition 1 (home-contractor model, where the unmeasured task is "very important", B(0, t_2) = 0): "the efficient linear compensation rule pays a fixed wage and contains no incentive component (α = 0), even if the contractor is risk neutral." Footnote 11 links this to Baker: "A related conclusion—that incorrect weightings of profit contributions in an accounting system lead the optimizing employer to weaken effort incentives—is derived by Baker." — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Proposition 2 (asset ownership / employment vs. contracting): "the optimal employment contract always entails paying a fixed wage (α = 0). Whenever the independent contracting relation is optimal, it involves 'high-powered incentives' (α > 0) ... If employment contracting is optimal for some fixed parameters (r, σ^2, σ_2^2), then it is also optimal for higher values of these parameters." The intro gives the resulting "rudimentary theory of ownership": the agent should own the asset when "(i) the agent is not too risk averse, (ii) the variance of asset returns is low, and (iii) the variance of measurement error in other aspects of the agent's performance is low. Thus, it emphasizes measurement cost as an important determinant of integration in contrast to the leading approaches, which stress asset specificity." — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Evidence cited by the paper: franchisees "face steep performance incentives, while managers of identical company-owned stores receive no incentive pay at all (Krueger, Brickley and Dark)"; firms "employ their own sales forces rather than independent manufacturer's representatives when some aspects of the representative's performance are hard to measure" (Anderson; Anderson and Schmittlein) — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Proposition 3 (bureaucratic constraints / outside activities): "The higher is the agent's marginal reward for performance in the main job, the greater is his freedom to pursue personal business"; "responsibility and authority should go hand in hand"; "Constraints are substitutes for performance incentives and are extensively used when it is hard to assess the performance of the agent"; and part (iii): "the social value of a personal activity, and the likelihood that it will be excluded, need bear little relationship to each other." — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Proposition 4(i): the optimal commission in the attention model is "α = p / [1 + r σ² (dt/dα)]" (OCR'd), and α and the set of permitted activities A(α) "are complementary instruments" — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Proposition 5 (job design): "it is never optimal for the two agents to be jointly responsible for any task k," because "providing incentives for an agent in any task incurs a fixed cost"; Proposition 6/Corollary: tasks should be grouped so that hard-to-measure tasks go together and easy-to-measure tasks go together, so the principal can "measure without fearing that the agent will substitute efforts away from other" tasks — [HM 1991](https://people.duke.edu/~qc2/BA532/1991%20JLEO%20Holmstrom%20Milgrom.pdf)
- Schmidt's textbook-style statement of the same result: "if the teacher's wage would depend on his students' test scores for basic skills, he would focus too much on teaching to the test and neglect developing other important skills ... if he is offered a fixed wage (and if there is some intrinsic motivation), he may work less hard altogether, but he would allocate his efforts more efficiently across tasks. Thus, if teaching creativity and social skills is sufficiently important, it is better to pay a fixed wage." Holmström-Milgrom (1991, 1994) also "show why some incentive instruments complement each other and should be used together" (ownership, high-powered pay, discretion co-vary) — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Prendergast's one-paragraph version: with costs depending only on total effort, "if the principal offers a contract with a higher return to one activity than the other, the agent allocates all his effort to one task ... In order to induce the agent to allocate time to both tasks, the same incentives must be offered on both. But there need be no reason why this is otherwise optimal; for instance, the measurement error on one (e.g., quantity produced) may be much lower than for the other (e.g., quality produced). As a result, multi-tasking imposes constraints on the trade-off between risk and incentives." He adds (fn. 27) that in HM "it is straightforward to show situations where the firm is better off offering no incentives. Essentially, this requires that agents be willing to supply" some effort without pay — [Prendergast 1999](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1999-prendergast.pdf)
- Prendergast attributes the term: "Following Bengt Holmstrom and Paul Milgrom (1990) and George Baker (1992), this incentive problem has become known as multi-tasking, where compensation on any subset of tasks will result in a reallocation of activities toward those that are directly compensated and away from the uncompensated activities." Prediction: "in those positions where there are significant opportunities for reallocation of activities, there will be an absence of pay-for-performance; in essence, complex jobs will typically not be evaluated through explicit contracts." And the Goodhart phrasing: "explicit contracts result in agents optimizing relative to the contract ('you get what you pay for')" — [Prendergast 1999](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1999-prendergast.pdf)

### Cited Findings — Baker 1992
- Baker, "Incentive Contracts and Performance Measurement," *Journal of Political Economy* 100(3), June 1992, pp. 598-614, DOI 10.1086/261831. Abstract: examines "incentive contracts in which the agent's payoff is not based on the principal's objective"; such contracts "generally fall short of first-best incentives, even when the agent is risk neutral"; the optimal contract's form and efficiency depend on how the performance measure relates to the principal's objective; applications to "gaming of performance measures, revenue-based sales commissions, and relative performance evaluation" — [IDEAS record of Baker 1992](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v100y1992i3p598-614.html)
- Prendergast's Baker-style model: true marginal product is e + α but the rewarded measure is ỹ = µe + α + ε_y with µ ~ N(1, σ_µ²) privately known, "where σ_µ² is a direct measure of the extent to which the agent can 'game' the compensation system." The agent chooses e* = µβ/c, "bases effort on a measure uncorrelated with social surplus," and the optimal piece rate is "β* = 1/(1 + σ_µ²) < 1 ... Thus, even with risk neutral agents, incentives are below unity in order to constrain inefficient behavioral responses." Prendergast notes this assumes, "As in Baker (1992)," that the firm cannot use revelation mechanisms — [Prendergast 1999](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1999-prendergast.pdf)

### Cited Findings — Baker, Gibbons & Murphy 1994
- Baker, Gibbons and Murphy, "Subjective Performance Measures in Optimal Incentive Contracts," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 109(4), Nov. 1994, pp. 1125-1156; NBER WP 4480 (1993) — [NBER w4480](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4480); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/oup/qjecon/v109y1994i4p1125-1156..html)
- Content (from NBER abstract): subjective measures are used in implicit (self-enforced) contracts, objective measures in explicit ones; "Objective and subjective measures often act as substitutes. If objective measures are close enough to perfect, no implicit contracts are feasible, because the firm's payoff from reneging becomes too attractive. Conversely, objective and subjective measures can reinforce each other. Better objective measures can raise the optimal weight on subjective measures, since they increase the value of the ongoing relationship and reduce the firm's incentive to renege." — [NBER w4480](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4480)
- Prendergast on why firms turn to subjective evaluation: "As a result of the danger of agents overemphasizing objective criteria, it is typically argued that firms should not pay based on objectively measured criteria, but instead should use subjective performance evaluation"; but subjective assessments bring "leniency bias," "centrality bias," and workers "currying favor with their bosses" — [Prendergast 1999](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1999-prendergast.pdf)
- Lazear, "Speeding, Tax Fraud, and Teaching to the Test," NBER WP 10932, Nov. 2004, is the standard applied treatment of test-based teacher incentives in the multitask spirit — [NBER w10932](http://www.nber.org/papers/w10932)

### Inferences
- The Goodhart's-law content of HM 1991 is precise: the distortion is not that the measure becomes noisy, but that the *agent reallocates attention* toward the measured dimension; the cure is to lower incentive intensity on what is measurable in proportion to how badly the competing tasks are measured (eq. 7), to redesign jobs so that measured and unmeasured tasks are held by different people (Props 5-6), or to use ownership and constraints as substitute instruments (Props 2-3).
- Baker 1992 isolates a different channel: a single effort, but a measure whose marginal sensitivity to effort (µ) differs from the objective's. Even without risk aversion, incentive intensity is 1/(1 + σ_µ²): the more the measure can be gamed, the flatter the optimal contract.
- BGM 1994 supplies the standard answer to "what do you do when every objective measure is gameable": combine it with a discretionary (subjective) component enforced by the value of the relationship, which is what links the multitask branch to the relational-contracts branch.

### Gaps

## Q4. Adverse selection and screening: Mirrlees, Mussa-Rosen, Baron-Myerson, Maskin-Riley, the revelation principle, commitment

### Takeaway
The screening branch is unified by the revelation principle (Myerson 1979): the set of outcomes achievable by any mechanism equals that achievable by direct, incentive-compatible mechanisms. The canonical applications are Mussa-Rosen 1978 (quality-price menus), Baron-Myerson 1982 (regulating a monopolist with unknown cost) and Maskin-Riley 1984 (nonlinear pricing), each producing the "no distortion at the top, downward distortion below, information rent" structure; commitment is the key assumption in the dynamic versions.

### Cited Findings
- Myerson, "Incentive Compatibility and the Bargaining Problem," *Econometrica* 47(1), Jan. 1979, pp. 61-74. The abstract: "the set of expected utility allocations which are feasible with incentive-compatible mechanisms is compact and convex," and this set "includes the equilibrium allocations of all other mechanisms" — [Econometric Society page for Myerson 1979](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1979/01/01/incentive-compatibility-and-bargaining-problem)
- Course statement of the principle: "any equilibrium outcome of any mechanism can also be implemented as the outcome of a direct mechanism" — [Aalto course material](https://mycourses.aalto.fi/mod/resource/view.php?id=1188602)
- Mussa and Rosen, "Monopoly and Product Quality," *Journal of Economic Theory* 18(2), 1978, pp. 301-317; "the ironing procedure of Mussa and Rosen is a standard technique for handling bunching" — [IDEAS record of Maskin-Riley 1984 and related](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/randje/v15y1984isummerp171-196.html)
- Baron and Myerson, "Regulating a Monopolist with Unknown Costs," *Econometrica* 50(4), 1982, pp. 911-930 — [IDEAS record of Baron-Myerson 1982](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v50y1982i4p911-30.html)
- Maskin and Riley, "Monopoly with Incomplete Information," *RAND Journal of Economics* 15(2), Summer 1984, pp. 171-196: "considers the case of a single principal (the monopolist) and shows that, under a separability assumption, strong conclusions can be drawn about the nature of optimal incentive schemes," with applications to "price discrimination via quantity discounts and monopoly pricing of products of differing quality (Mussa and Rosen, 1978)" — [IDEAS record of Maskin-Riley 1984](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/randje/v15y1984isummerp171-196.html)
- Laffont and Martimort's textbook treats adverse selection first and devotes a chapter to "dynamics under full commitment," marking commitment as the organising assumption for dynamic screening — [oeaw.ac.at record](https://oeaw.ac.at/resources/Record/9781400829453)
- Prendergast notes the ratchet effect as a career-concern-type phenomenon and that "ratchet effects" arise when the principal learns the agent's type from performance and cannot commit — [Prendergast 1999](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1999-prendergast.pdf)

### Inferences
- The newcomer's map: Mirrlees' 1971 optimal-income-tax problem (not retrieved here) is the ancestor; Mussa-Rosen, Baron-Myerson and Maskin-Riley are the three "one-principal, one-dimensional type" workhorses; the revelation principle collapses the design space to a menu indexed by type; information rents accrue to higher types and quantity/quality is distorted downward for all but the top type (the "efficiency at the top" property, stated in every textbook but not quoted from a source here).
- Commitment: with full commitment the static solution repeats; without it, the ratchet effect and renegotiation (Laffont-Tirole 1988; Dewatripont 1989) break the revelation principle's convenience. These are standard textbook facts (Laffont-Martimort ch. 8-9; Bolton-Dewatripont ch. 9) but were not retrieved as primary sources in this session.

- Mirrlees 1971 (Review of Economic Studies, optimal income taxation) and Laffont-Tirole 1986/1988/1993 were not retrieved; a follow-up search is needed for the dynamic-screening and ratchet-effect literature.

## Q5. Incomplete contracts: Grossman-Hart-Moore property rights, Hart-Moore 1999, the Maskin-Tirole critique

### Takeaway
Grossman-Hart 1986 and Hart-Moore 1990 define a firm by the assets it owns, define ownership as residual control rights, and show that with non-contractible relationship-specific investments and ex post Nash/Shapley bargaining, ownership should go to the party whose investment matters most, complementary assets should be owned together, and indispensable agents should own. Maskin-Tirole 1999 proved an "irrelevance theorem": if parties can foresee payoffs, indescribability of states does not matter, because a revelation mechanism restores the first best; Hart-Moore 1999 reply that the mechanism requires commitment not to renegotiate.

### Cited Findings
- Grossman and Hart, "The Costs and Benefits of Ownership: A Theory of Vertical and Lateral Integration," *Journal of Political Economy* 94(4), 1986, pp. 691-719 — [Kóczy & Kiss 2017](https://hitelintezetiszemle.mnb.hu/en/laszlo-a-koczy-hubert-janos-kiss)
- Hart and Moore, "Property Rights and the Nature of the Firm," *Journal of Political Economy* 98(6), 1990, pp. 1119-1158, DOI 10.1086/261729; "provides a framework for addressing the question of when transactions should be carried out within a firm and when through the market"; "identifies a firm with the assets that its owners control" — [Hart's publication page](https://hart.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/property-rights-and-nature-firm); [DASH copy](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/3448675/2/Hart_PropertyRights.pdf)
- Residual control rights: "Grossman and Hart (1986) distinguish between 'specific control rights' and 'residual control rights'. Specific control rights can be contracted upon ... If, however, a contingency has not been dealt with by the contract, then the party with the residual rights of control decides what is to be done ... the owner of an asset can exclude other parties from using the asset. Grossman and Hart define a 'firm' as the set of physical assets that the firm controls." — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- The problem solved: the "Williamson puzzle" — "a merger must always (weakly) increase efficiency if the involved parties can engage in 'selective intervention'." GH 1986 "offer a formal model that solves this puzzle." Mechanism: "The only contracts that are feasible ex ante ... are contracts on the allocation of ownership rights"; bargaining "result[s] in the Nash bargaining solution"; the owner "can exclude the other party from using the assets," so "the owner will get a larger share of the surplus and thus has a stronger incentive to invest." Result: "if the relationship specific investments of one party are very important while the investments of the other party are not, then the first party should own the assets (integration). If, however, the investments of both parties are of similar importance, then it is better that each party owns one of the assets (non-integration)." "The benefit of integration is that the owner of the firm has a stronger incentive to invest. The cost is that the investment incentives of the non-owner are reduced." — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Hart-Moore 1990 generalisation: "N parties, K assets and a rich set of possible ownership structures ... ex post bargaining is always efficient ... and results in a division of the ex post surplus that is given by the Shapley value ... it shows that complementary assets should be owned together, that not more than one party should have veto power over an asset, that if an agent is indispensable for an asset, then this agent should own the asset, and that if a group of agents is indispensable for a set of assets, then they should control these assets by simple majority vote." — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Lecture-note formalisation (Hubbard, Northwestern): two managers invest i and e; surplus R(i) − C(e) with trade, r(i) − c(e) without; each gets half the gains from trade; R'(i) > r'(i) so investments are partly specific; "Because neither manager captures the full value of his investment, both underinvest relative to the first best." Ownership rules: if one party's investment is inelastic, the other should own; if one party's human capital is essential, that party should own both assets; independent assets should be separately owned; complementary assets should be owned by one party — [Hubbard lecture 15](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/hubbard/ec174/lectures/15prop.htm)
- Why contracts cannot specify everything (the Hart-Moore view, per Schmidt): parties "are unable to specify all possible contingencies in a contract, for example because the contingencies cannot be described or verified to the courts" — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Maskin and Tirole, "Unforeseen Contingencies and Incomplete Contracts," *Review of Economic Studies* 66(1), 1999, pp. 83-114. Abstract: "The literature usually assumes that contractual incompleteness is due to the transaction costs of describing, or of even foreseeing, the possible states of nature in advance. We argue, however, that such transaction costs need not interfere with optimal contracting, provided agents can forecast payoffs"; the "irrelevance theorem": indescribability does not matter "as long as there is symmetric information at both the contracting stage and the trading stage"; "The foregoing optimality result holds very generally provided that parties can commit themselves not to renegotiate. However, even if renegotiation is allowed, the result still remains valid provided that parties are risk averse." — [IDEAS record of Maskin-Tirole 1999](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v66y1999i1p83-114..html); [Harvard PDF](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/maskin/files/unforeseen_contingencies_and_incomplete_contracts.pdf)
- Schmidt's gloss: "if the parties correctly anticipate their payoffs in the different states of the world, then it is possible to construct a revelation mechanism that induces them to announce the state of the world truthfully. Such a mechanism can be used to implement the first best efficient allocation independently of the allocation of ownership rights." — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Hart and Moore, "Foundations of Incomplete Contracts," *Review of Economic Studies* 66(1), 1999, pp. 115-138: "we evaluate some of the criticisms that have been made of the theory, in particular, those in Maskin and Tirole (1998a). In doing so, we develop a model that provides a rigorous foundation for the idea that contracts are incomplete." — [LSE Research Online record](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/19354). Schmidt: "Hart and Moore (1999) argue that the Maskin-Tirole mechanism requires that the parties can commit not to renegotiate the outcome of the mechanism. They offer an example (based on Segal 1999) in which the efficient allocation cannot be implemented if renegotiation cannot be excluded." — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Status of the debate: "The debate on the theoretical foundations of incomplete contracts is not yet settled. Sophisticated revelation mechanisms such as the one proposed by Maskin and Tirole (1999) are not observed in the real world, but we do not yet understand why. One possible answer is that these mechanisms are very sensitive to small changes in the informational structure. For example, Aghion et al. (2012) show that subgame perfect implementation (Moore and Repullo, 1988) relies on a very strong common knowledge assumption." — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Other foundations results named by Schmidt: "Aghion, Dewatripont and Rey (1994) also show that the first best can be achieved if it is possible ex ante to allocate all the bargaining power to one party. On the other hand Che and Hausch (1999) show that it is impossible to achieve efficient investments if the investments are 'co-operative'." — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Behavioural response: Hart and Moore, "Contracts as Reference Points," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* (2008) "develop a behavioral model of incomplete contracts that is not prone to the critique of Maskin and Tirole (1999)"; parties feel "entitled to the best possible outcome that is consistent with the contract" and if denied it they "shade" in non-contractible ways; the traditional model, by always reaching ex post efficiency, makes it "hard to see why authority, hierarchy, delegation, or indeed anything apart from asset ownership matters" (HM 2008, p. 3) — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Applications: Hart, Shleifer and Vishny, "The Proper Scope of Government: Theory and an Application to Prisons," *QJE* 112(4), 1997, pp. 1127-1161; Hart and Moore, "Default and Renegotiation: A Dynamic Model of Debt," *QJE* 113(1), 1998, pp. 1-41 — [Kóczy & Kiss 2017](https://hitelintezetiszemle.mnb.hu/en/laszlo-a-koczy-hubert-janos-kiss). Schmidt on HSV: the private owner-manager "gets all the benefits from a cost reduction (without internalizing the negative externality due to lower quality) but only some fraction of the benefits from quality improvements. Therefore, he will invest too much in cost reduction and too little in quality improvement" — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Maskin's 2002 restatement: "as long as risk-averse parties can foresee the probabilities of their possible payoffs, then the fact that they cannot describe the possible physical states does not matter" — [Maskin, On Indescribable Contingencies (Harvard PDF)](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/maskin/files/on_indescribable_contingencies_and_incomplete_contracts_e._maskin.pdf)

### Inferences
- The incomplete-contracts branch answers a different question from the moral-hazard branch: not "how to pay" but "who should decide when the contract is silent." The theory's whole predictive content lives in the mapping from investment importance / asset complementarity / indispensability to ownership.
- The Maskin-Tirole critique is a mechanism-design attack: if everything relevant is foreseeable in payoff terms, message games substitute for descriptions. The Hart-Moore defence rests on renegotiation (and, later, on behavioural shading). This remains the main open foundational issue through 2021.

- The exact title and pages of Grossman-Hart 1986 and Hart-Moore 1990 are verified; the original-paper propositions were not read directly (the DASH PDF was not fetched).
- Segal 1999 (complexity and renegotiation), Moore-Repullo 1988 (subgame perfect implementation) and Aghion et al. 2012 were seen only as citations inside Schmidt.

## Q6. Relational and dynamic contracts: MacLeod-Malcomson, Levin 2003, Rogerson 1985, Fudenberg-Holmström-Milgrom 1990, Sannikov 2008

### Takeaway
Relational contracts replace court enforcement with the value of continuing the relationship (MacLeod-Malcomson 1989, Levin 2003: stationary optimal contracts, two-level pay under moral hazard, termination after poor subjective reviews). Dynamic moral hazard adds memory and consumption-smoothing (Rogerson 1985: the inverse-Euler condition and the need to restrict the agent's savings; FHM 1990: when short-term contracts replicate long-term ones). Sannikov 2008 makes the agent's continuation value the state variable of an ODE in continuous time.

### Cited Findings
- MacLeod and Malcomson, "Implicit Contracts, Incentive Compatibility, and Involuntary Unemployment," *Econometrica* 57(2), March 1989, pp. 447-480: "Implementation requires a strictly positive surplus from employment"; "Piece-rate contracts, and contracts with an informally agreed bonus, can be made self-enforcing"; severance pay and bonding do not enlarge the implementable set; "Unfilled vacancies and unemployed workers can co-exist despite the existence of contracts that are potentially mutually beneficial" — [Econometric Society page](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1989/03/01/implicit-contracts-incentive-compatibility-and-involuntary)
- Levin, "Relational Incentive Contracts," *AER* 93(3), June 2003, pp. 835-857: "optimal contracts often can take a simple stationary form," "self-enforcement restricts promised compensation and affects incentive provision. With hidden information, it may be optimal for an agent to supply the same inefficient effort regardless of cost conditions. With moral hazard, optimal contracts involve just two levels of compensation. This is true even if performance measures are subjective, in which case optimal contracts terminate following poor performance." — [IDEAS record of Levin 2003](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v93y2003i3p835-857.html)
- Rogerson, "Repeated Moral Hazard," *Econometrica* 53(1), Jan. 1985, pp. 69-76: risk-neutral principal, risk-averse agent, fixed interest rate; "Memory plays a very strong role in every Pareto-optimal contract"; "limiting the agent's access to credit is necessary to reach a Pareto-optimal outcome" — [Econometric Society page for Rogerson 1985 (repeated)](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1985/01/01/repeated-moral-hazard)
- Fudenberg, Holmström and Milgrom, "Short-Term Contracts and Long-Term Agency Relationships," *Journal of Economic Theory* 51(1), 1990, pp. 1-31 (MIT WP 1987, first draft Jan. 1986) — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/r/eee/jetheo/v51y1990i1p1-31.html)
- Sannikov, "A Continuous-Time Version of the Principal-Agent Problem," *Review of Economic Studies* 75, 2008, pp. 957-984: output "is a diffusion process whose drift the agent's unobserved effort determines"; the risk-averse agent consumes continuously; the solution uses "the agent's continuation value as the state variable and is computed from a differential equation," which the paper presents as its main technical contribution; the continuation value "evolves with output until it hits a boundary that ends employment, such as retirement, quitting, replacement, or promotion" — [Sannikov 2008 PDF copy](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/sannikov-the-review-of-economic-studies-2008A.pdf)
- Scrutiny: Possamaï and Touzi, "Is there a Golden Parachute in Sannikov's principal-agent problem?" (arXiv 2007.05529) re-examine with different discount rates and find golden-parachute contracts "exist only in special cases, which contradicts Sannikov's 2008 claim that they arise whenever the agent's marginal cost of effort is positive at zero" — [arXiv 2007.05529](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.05529). A CIRJE working paper notes Sannikov "assumes the principal can observe the agent's consumption" — [CIRJE 2021](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/dp/2021/2021cf1182.pdf)
- MacLeod and Malcomson's 2023 retrospective ("Thirty Years On," *JITE* 179) says the 1989 paper "remains our most highly cited work" — [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/implicit-contracts-incentive-compatibility-and-involuntary-unempl/)
- Schmidt on the HM 1987 dynamic model's domain: it "considers an agent working in a stationary environment. Thus, it is not suitable for the analysis of the optimal contract for a top manager, but rather applies to workers and mid-level employees, ... salespeople, and independent contractors" — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)

### Inferences
- The two "dynamic" literatures should be kept apart for a newcomer: (a) *repeated moral hazard with commitment* (Rogerson 1985, FHM 1990, Sannikov 2008), where the issue is intertemporal smoothing and the role of history; (b) *relational contracts without enforcement* (MacLeod-Malcomson 1989, BGM 1994, Levin 2003), where the issue is the dynamic enforcement constraint — the principal must prefer paying the promised bonus to reneging and losing the relationship.
- Levin's two-level (bonus or no bonus) result and termination-after-bad-review are the formal counterpart of BGM 1994's subjective-measure contracts and are the standard model of discretionary bonuses.

## Q7. Robust contracts: Carroll 2015 and the max-min approach

### Takeaway
Carroll (AER 105(2), 2015) shows that if the principal knows only some of the agent's available actions, is risk-neutral, faces limited liability, and judges contracts by their worst-case payoff over unknown actions, then a linear contract (a fixed share of output) is optimal, because the only guarantee the principal can leverage is a lower bound on the agent's payoff, and linear contracts turn that bound into a bound on her own payoff with equality.

### Cited Findings
- Carroll, "Robustness and Linear Contracts," *American Economic Review* 105(2), 2015, pp. 536-563, DOI 10.1257/aer.20131159. Abstract: "the principal is uncertain as to what the agent can and cannot do: she knows some actions available to the agent, but other, unknown actions may also exist. The principal demands robustness, evaluating possible contracts by their worst-case performance, over unknown actions the agent might potentially take. The model assumes risk-neutrality and limited liability, and no other functional form assumptions. Very generally, the optimal contract is linear." — [Carroll 2015 PDF](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/gabriel.carroll/robustcontract-published.pdf)
- The principal "does not even have a prior belief about these unknown actions. In this nonprobabilistic setting, we assume a simple (arguably the simplest) criterion to evaluate contracts: any contract is judged by its worst possible performance, given the principal's knowledge." — [Carroll 2015](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/gabriel.carroll/robustcontract-published.pdf)
- Theorem 1: "There exists a linear contract that maximizes V_P" (the principal's guarantee); Corollary 1: "If A_0 satisfies the full-support condition, then every contract that maximizes V_P is linear"; Theorem 2 (variant with a participation/outside option): an optimal contract exists and is affine — [Carroll 2015](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/gabriel.carroll/robustcontract-published.pdf)
- Intuition in the author's words: "the one thing she does know is a lower bound on the agent's expected payoff (from the actions that are known to be available). The only effective way to turn this into a lower bound on her own expected payoff is via a linear relationship between the two ... Even when a contract is nonlinear, whatever guarantee it gives is still driven by a linear relationship, which in general is an inequality. Linear contracts are the ones for which this relationship is tight, and this is why they are optimal." Worked example: a one-third share and a known action worth 400 to the agent guarantees the principal at least 800 — [Carroll 2015](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/gabriel.carroll/robustcontract-published.pdf)
- Positioning: "it addresses a longstanding problem in contract theory: why are linear contracts common in practice, while textbook models often predict more complicated functional forms? As Holmström ..." (the sentence continues into the standard Holmström-Milgrom 1987 reference); the guarantee calculation "appears also in Chassang 2013, Theorem 1" — [Carroll 2015](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/gabriel.carroll/robustcontract-published.pdf)
- Downstream (pre-2022): Walton and Carroll (*Econometrica* 90(5), 2022, pp. 2129-2159) extend the guarantee argument to counterparties with internal organisational structure — [ECTA17386 PDF](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/gabriel.carroll/ECTA17386.pdf); Carroll's "general linear" working paper (2019) generalises further — [general-linear-051819 PDF](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/gabriel.carroll/general-linear-051819.pdf)

### Inferences
- Carroll's result is the third canonical rationale for linear contracts, after HM 1987 (rich action space, CARA-normal) and the aggregation argument; unlike HM 1987 it needs no risk aversion or distributional assumptions, only non-quantifiable uncertainty about the agent's technology and a worst-case criterion.
- For the Goodhart theme, robustness answers "what if the agent can do things I did not model": linear sharing is the contract that cannot be exploited by unknown actions, because any action that helps the agent helps the principal proportionally.

### Gaps
- Chassang 2013 (*AER*, "Calibrated Incentive Contracts") was seen only as cited inside Carroll; not independently retrieved.
- The pre-Carroll max-min literature (Hurwicz-Shapiro 1978 on sharecropping; Diamond 1998) was not searched.

## Q8. Career concerns and reputation as implicit contracts: Holmström 1982/1999, Gibbons-Murphy 1992, teams

### Takeaway
Holmström's career-concerns model (1982 Essays in Honor of Lars Wahlbeck; republished *REStud* 66(1), 1999, pp. 169-182) shows that a competitive labour market paying expected productivity creates an implicit incentive contract: the agent works hard early to influence the market's inference about ability, even though in equilibrium the market is not fooled, and effort declines over the career. Gibbons-Murphy 1992 show explicit incentives should be strongest when career concerns are weakest (near retirement). Holmström 1982 "Moral Hazard in Teams" shows budget-balancing sharing rules cannot achieve efficiency, giving the principal a role as budget-breaker.

### Cited Findings
- Holmström, "Managerial Incentive Problems: A Dynamic Perspective," *Review of Economic Studies* 66(1), 1999, pp. 169-182 (NBER WP 6875); abstract: wages are set on expected output, so "an implicit contract connects today's performance to future pay, even without explicit output-contingent contracts"; "The incentive problem comes from the person's ability and desire to influence the learning process, and therefore the wage process, through unobserved actions"; career motives "can be beneficial or detrimental, depending on how well the two kinds of capital returns are aligned" — [NBER W6875](https://www.nber.org/papers/W6875); [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=227626)
- Schmidt: "Fama (1980) argued that the moral hazard problem may be solved by career concerns and that performance-based wage contracts are therefore unnecessary. Holmström (1982b) offers the first formal model of career concerns." Mechanism: "the agent works very hard at the beginning of his career, possibly spending an effort level that is far higher than the first best level of effort. Over time his effort level falls because there are fewer and fewer periods left that can be affected. The model nicely captures a 'rat race effect' ... because the market expects him to work so hard, it attributes the agent's output to his high effort and not to high ability. Nevertheless the agent cannot reduce his efforts." Footnote 7: the model assumes symmetric information about ability between agent and market — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf)
- Gibbons and Murphy, "Optimal Incentive Contracts in the Presence of Career Concerns: Theory and Evidence," *Journal of Political Economy* 100(3), 1992, pp. 468-505: "the optimal pay contract optimizes total incentives, combining implicit career-concern incentives with explicit pay incentives," and "the explicit incentives from the optimal compensation contract should be strongest for workers close to retirement" — [IDEAS record of Gibbons-Murphy 1992](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v100y1992i3p468-505.html)
- Prendergast on career concerns: as performance measures "become noisier, career concerns fall," and "career concerns can harm incentives" when they induce myopia or distortion; the baseball example: a player on a fixed salary still exerts effort "to affect future contracts, which depend on current performance" — [Prendergast 1999](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1999-prendergast.pdf)
- Holmström, "Moral Hazard in Teams," *Bell Journal of Economics* 13(2), Autumn 1982, pp. 324-340: "The free-rider problem implies a new role for the principal: administering incentive schemes that do not balance the budget" — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/bellje/v13y1982iautumnp324-340.html). Schmidt: with only a team signal "a public good problem arises. Each agent has an incentive to free-ride on the efforts of the other team members"; the paper also introduces relative performance evaluation — [Schmidt 2017](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58031/1/19.pdf). Rasmusen (RAND 1987) notes the budget-breaking result "holds for risk-neutral agents and is not generally true when agents are risk averse" — [Rasmusen 1987 text](https://rasmusen.org/xpacioli/published/Rasmusen_87RJE.teams.txt)

### Inferences
- Career concerns are the field's main model of "implicit contracts via reputation": the market's Bayesian updating plays the role the explicit likelihood-ratio contract plays in Holmström 1979, which is why the two are usually taught back-to-back.
- The Gibbons-Murphy substitutability result (explicit incentives ∝ 1 − career-concern strength) is the standard empirical hook.
